# xLightGCN: Explainable Graph Neural Network Based Recommendation Systems with Most Influential Peers 

# Calder Johnson Robin Cohen 

Computer Science, University of Waterloo, Canada cbjohnson@uwaterloo.ca rcohen@uwaterloo.ca 

Abstract 

Recommendation systems, systems that predict a user’s pref-erence for items in order to curate that user’s feed, are a ubiq-uitous and central feature in online social networks. While they are a central piece of our modern digital infrastructure, many modern approaches are opaque black boxes, making them difficult to audit, understand, and assess the trustwor-thiness of. In this paper we present an exact, graph-based approach for generating explanations for decisions made by recommendation systems. We argue that in the risk-sensitive sociotechnical context of user-facing AI such as recommen-dation systems, explainability is a key pillar for establishing the trustworthiness of models and validating their effective-ness. We propose a technique built on LightGCN-like archi-tectures, an influential and popular class of recommendation models. We describe our approach and argue that it provides an appropriate assurance mechanism for these AI systems leading to specific advantages, including interpretable expla-nations and a theoretically guaranteed measure of explanation fidelity. We conclude by discussing limitations and directions for future research.

# Introduction 

Recommendation systems have become a central component of modern digital infrastructure. Online platforms use them to determine which products, social media posts, advertise-ments, etc., are shown to individual users. By predicting user preferences and recommending only a small subset of poten-tially relevant items from the full space of possibilities, rec-ommendation systems can substantially improve the usabil-ity and engagement of online platforms. At the same time, the increasing role of automated recommendation in deter-mining what users encounter creates an important challenge for trustworthy AI: the decisions of these systems must be understandable and auditable by the developers and organi-zations responsible for them. Graph-based recommendation systems are particularly ef-fective at exploiting the relational structure inherent in user behavior. A recommendation dataset can naturally be rep-resented as a bipartite graph in which users and items are vertices and interactions such as purchases, views, or clicks form edges. This representation allows information to prop-agate through chains of interactions, enabling a model to exploit higher-order collaborative relationships that are dif-ficult to represent using conventional recommendation ap-proaches. LightGCN (He et al. 2020) is an influential archi-tecture in this paradigm, employing a simplified GCN (graph convolutional network) with no feature transformations and nonlinear activation functions while achieving strong empir-ical performance. Given a set of users, a set of items, and the graph of past interactions between them, LightGCN predicts the affinity between a given user u and item i by producing initial node embeddings H0 and running them through L convolutional layers. Despite their power, the opacity of these models presents an important assurance problem. A recommendation de-pends on complex chains of interactions involving other users and items that provide a rich collaborative signal to the model but are intractable for humans to inspect at the scale of a real interaction graph. Moreover, interaction histo-ries can encode highly personal, detailed information about arXiv:2610.19446v1 [cs.AI] 14 Oct 2026 user behavior and preferences. A misaligned recommenda-tion model operating on sensitive user data whose decision making process is opaque presents significant risk from a sociotechnical perspective. For example, without an appro-priate assurance framework in place, such a system could promote echo chambers, give identity-biased recommenda-tions, or hurt brand image by targeting ads to the wrong de-mographics. We view explainability as a mechanism for producing ev-idence that can support the assessment of AI-system trust-worthiness. By exposing the evidence underlying a deci-sion, developers and auditors can inspect model behavior and identify potentially problematic dependencies, allowing them to make an informed assessment of trustworthiness. The ability to identify which relationships contributed to a recommendation is therefore key to the transparency, risk as-sessment, and trustworthiness judgement of user-facing AI systems. Existing explanation methods for GNNs often produce approximate or computationally expensive explanations. GNNExplainer (Ying et al. 2019), for example, identifies in-formative subsets of a graph through an optimization pro-cedure that masks nodes and edges while attempting to preserve the model’s prediction. SubgraphX (Yuan et al. 2021) instead uses a search procedure based on Monte Carlo Tree Search and Shapley values to identify influential sub-graphs. Recommendation-specific approaches have also pro-duced interpretable reasoning chains through knowledge graphs (Wang et al. 2019, 2018). These methods demon-strate the feasibility of generating human-interpretable ex-planations, but they generally either rely on additional op-timization or search procedures for individual predictions. This motivates the question of whether the linear structure of LightGCN can be exploited to obtain explanations that are both computationally efficient and directly grounded in the model’s actual computation. In this work, we propose xLightGCN, an explanation framework that identifies the most influential peers underly-ing individual LightGCN recommendations. Using the lin-ear structure of LightGCN, we precisely isolate the compact explanatory subgraph that explains most of the model’s pre-diction in an efficient manner. In addition, the component of the prediction explained by the subgraph can be compared to the complete prediction to quantify the contribution left unexplained by the extracted subgraph, providing a theoret-ically grounded measure of explanation fidelity.

# Experiments 

We ran xLightGCN on the Gowalla dataset (https://snap.stanford.edu/data/loc-gowalla.html), a large scale benchmark dataset for recommendation systems where users are recommended geographical locations (points of interest). We tested across a range of possible 

k values to assess the relationship between k and fidelity score (Figure 2), each averaged across 100 randomly chosen user/item pairs, choosing the k range to be between 0 and 100, as any larger would be a dense subgraph difficult to interpret. We also assess the size of the explanatory subgraph re-quired to reach a certain fidelity threshold on average, (Fig-ure 3). These results show a tradeoff between explanatory sub-graph size k and fidelity F (u, i ): Larger subgraphs explain more of the model’s prediction but are more difficult to in-terpret due to their complexity. The relationship between fidelity and k shows diminishing improvements as k in-creases, suggesting that a few influential nodes are key to ex-plaining most of the prediction, providing empirical support for the effectiveness of xLightGCN for isolating a small, in-terpretable subgraph that explains most of a recommenda-tion system’s prediction. 

# Conclusion 

In this paper, we presented xLightGCN, a model-specific framework for generating compact explanations of recom-mendations produced by LightGCN family architectures. xLightGCN identifies the nodes that most strongly influence an individual recommendation and represents these contri-butions as an interpretable subgraph, providing evidence grounded in the actual mechanism producing the prediction. Furthermore, the unexplained residual and resulting fidelity score provide a quantitative measure of how completely the explanation accounts for the model’s output. The explana-tions produced by xLightGCN together with a quantitative indication of the coverage of that explanation provide evi-dence that can support transparency and auditing, and can assist developers in evaluating the trustworthiness of their recommendation systems. The efficiency gains generated by our method also provide added-value. Our work illustrates a broader role for explainability in trustworthy AI: explainability can serve as more than a user-facing interpretability feature; it can be an assurance mecha-nism for identifying, auditing, and quantifying the evidence underlying model outputs. Several limitations remain. Most importantly, an expla-nation does not inherently mean that a system is trustwor-thy or accurate, it just provides a tool through which these properties can be investigated. In addition, xLightGCN re-lies on the linear propagation structure of LightGCN and is therefore not applicable directly to arbitrary GCN or rec-ommendation architectures. This specialization is what en-ables its exact and efficient decomposition, suggesting that architecture-specific explainability can be valuable when strong assurances about model behavior are required. Future work should investigate explainability frameworks in other architectures with tractable computational struc-tures. Also, the paradigm of isolating influential nodes in order to understand complex network behavior should be explored further; the work done on Dynamic Opinion Net-works (Muppasani et al. 2024a), (Muppasani et al. 2024b), may provide further valuable insights. In addition, we ar-gue that explainability offers strong insight in assessing the trustworthiness of AI systems, and more research needs to be done exploring its role in developing trustworthy, safe, and assured AI.
