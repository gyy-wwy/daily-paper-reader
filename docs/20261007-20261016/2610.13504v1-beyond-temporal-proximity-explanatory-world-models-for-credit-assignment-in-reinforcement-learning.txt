ABSTRACT 

Generative Recommenders increasingly use Reinforcement Learning (RL) to op-timize outcomes that may appear hundreds or thousands of interactions after a decision. Standard RL propagates reward according to temporal proximity rather than explicit relevance, attenuating the signal assigned to distant but important ac-tions and creating a source of sample inefficiency. We introduce Explanatory World Models (EWM), an attribution framework that uses explanations to identify the earlier events that plausibly contributed to a realized outcome. We instantiate EWM with a frontier language model and use its explanations as learning signals using a conservative hierarchical operator that recursively redistributes reward while preserving the undiscounted episode return. To investigate what constitutes a useful explanation, we operationalize three complementary variants inspired by Halpern’s actual causation, Lombrozo’s explanatory simplicity, and Deutsch’s hard-to-vary explanations. We evaluate EWM on two controlled long-horizon RL benchmarks, a public sequential-recommendation benchmark, and an industry-scale generative recommender. On Crafter and WordCraft, EWM substantially outperforms PPO and LaRe, with its largest gains when rewards are most delayed. On KuaiRand, EWM outperforms SFT and LaRe offline and A2C online. Finally, in an offline evaluation of an industry generative recommender, EWM considerably improves Recall and increases behavioral proxies. These results suggest that explanations can provide structured and reusable credit for learning from delayed outcomes. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

In long-horizon decision-making problems, the consequences of an action may become apparent only after hundreds or thousands of subsequent decisions. An agent might gather a resource early in an episode but use it hundreds of steps later to complete an achievement spanning thousands of steps. For example, a podcast recommendation surfaced today may introduce a listener to a show, shape their consumption over the following weeks, and ultimately affect whether they remain active months later. A recommender optimized to increase activity must determine which actions deserve credit. This is the problem of temporal credit assignment (Minsky, 1961; Sutton & Barto, 2018). Standard RL algorithms propagate reward backward based on temporal proximity, i.e., assigning more credit to recent actions through discounted returns. This mechanism is computationally convenient, but it embeds a temporal prior: actions closer to a reward are treated as more relevant. Given enough trajectories, an agent may learn to average out these spurious associations and recover the true relations between actions and reward. Doing so requires learning the structure of the environment almost entirely through trial and error. The length of the delay is therefore translated into sample inefficiency, where the further the action, the more interactions are required to identify its contribution. A substantial body of work improves on raw discounted returns through return decomposition, hindsight information, or counterfactual reasoning (Arjona-Medina et al., 2019; Patil et al., 2020; Harutyunyan et al., 2019; Mesnard et al., 2021). These approaches demonstrate that credit does not need to be assigned solely according to the original reward timing. Rather than requiring the policy’s critic to discover relevance only through future-return prediction, we pursue a complementary

Cognitive and social scientists treat explanations as central to human inquiry and cognition: explanations organize scientific under-standing, drive theory formation, and shape learning, causal inference, and generalization (Hempel & Oppenheim, 1948; Gopnik, 1998; Keil, 2006; Lombrozo, 2006; Felekis et al., 2026; Gilligan-Lee, 2020; Reynaud et al., 2022). Different accounts of explanation emphasize different criteria for identi-fying which preceding events matter (Lombrozo, 2006). Halpern and Pearl ground explanation in actual causation (Halpern & Pearl, 2005); Lombrozo emphasizes explanatory simplicity (Lombrozo, 2007); and Deutsch argues that good explanations are hard to vary and possess reach beyond the phenomenon for which they were constructed (Deutsch, 2011). We present Explanatory World Models (EWM) , a framework that attributes an observed event/re-ward to the earlier events/actions that best explain it. Given a trajectory and an event within it, an EWM returns a weighted set of preceding events. A redistribution operator then transports the reward to those events, changing where the learning signal is applied while preserving the episode’s return. EWM therefore modifies neither the environment nor the policy class; it inserts an attribution step between observing a reward and updating the policy. To convert explanations into a learning signal, we introduce a conservative hierarchical redistribution operator . Explanations provide plausible causes to an outcome; instead of following a temporal bias, we can follow those relations to reallocate value to them. Since EWM explains outcomes, not just rewards, we can apply this process recursively allowing to optimize value for extremely long horizons. Moreover, a key benefit of explanation-based redistribution is its independence from the reward function. Changing the reward function therefore changes how an outcome is valued, but not which preceding events explain it, allowing explanations to be reused across reward functions. We instantiate EWM with a frontier language model, using its prior knowledge to explain observed outcomes without having to learn the environment’s relations from interaction alone. We evaluate EWM in both long-horizon reasoning and real-world recommendation settings. On Crafter, EWM outperforms PPO across all eight tested combinations of discount factor and episode-length cap. On WordCraft, EWM outperforms PPO (Schulman et al., 2017) on the harder splits. In the offline KuaiRand setting, EWM outperforms SFT and LaRe. In the online KuaiRand setting, EWM out-performs PPO and achieves performance competitive with an oracle method. In an offline replay evaluation of an industry-scale generative recommender, RLFT with EWM increases recall and independent measures of future engagement by 3× over the SFT baseline, indicating that its gains extend beyond reproducing logged recommendations to downstream engagement proxies.

2 RELATED WORK 

Credit assignment and reward redistribution. Standard RL propagates reward through boot-strapped or discounted returns (Sutton & Barto, 2018; Schulman et al., 2016). Prior work bridges long temporal gaps using learned connections to relevant memories (Ke et al., 2018; Hung et al., 2019), return decomposition (Arjona-Medina et al., 2019; Patil et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2023), and outcome-conditioned or counterfactual attribution (Harutyunyan et al., 2019; Mesnard et al., 2021; Meulemans et al., 2023). EWM shares the objective of assigning credit to relevant earlier decisions, but derives relevance from a prior-informed explanation of the realized trajectory rather than learning an attribution or return model exclusively from task interactions. It then conservatively redistributes the observed reward and applies an otherwise standard RL algorithm. 

Explanations and language-model rewards. Research in philosophy and cognitive science treats explanation as a mechanism for identifying the conditions responsible for an observed phenomenon and for supporting learning and causal inference (Hempel & Oppenheim, 1948; Gopnik, 1998; Keil, 2006; Lombrozo, 2006; Miller, 2019). Language models have also been used to generate reward functions and intrinsic goals (Ma et al., 2024; Du et al., 2023). Most closely, LaRe uses an LLM to construct a latent-reward representation and learns a decoder that maps it to episodic returns (Qu et al., 2025). EWM instead asks the model which observed events explain a realized outcome, grounds its attributions in the trajectory, and reallocates an externally specified reward without defining a new reward function. 

World models and long-term recommendation. Conventional world models learn forward dy-namics for planning or imagined rollouts (Ha & Schmidhuber, 2018; Hafner et al., 2021; 2023), while successor features separate reward-independent dynamics from task-specific value (Barreto et al., 2017). EWM is backward-looking and represents reward-independent explanatory relations among observed events. This attribution is complementary to work on long-term and generative recommendation, which primarily studies policy architecture, action representation, user modeling, and long-term value estimation (Van Goffrier et al., 2023; Deffayet et al., 2023; Gao et al., 2022). 

3 EXPLANATORY WORLD MODELS 

This section presents Explanatory World Models and their use for credit assignment. We first define the general framework of explanations, then introduce the conservative hierarchical reward redistribution operator that translates explanations into learning signals, describe frontier models as a practical EWM instantiation, and finally integrate EWM to online and offline RL algorithms. 3.1 WHAT IS AN EXPLANATION ?Explanations take many forms, making a single definition difficult. Miller (2019) describes ex-planation as an abductive process: given an observed event, or phenomenon , an explainer infers a hypothesis about the causes that brought it about. The credit-assignment problem is the challenge of determining which actions, or sequences of actions, contributed to an observed outcome (Minsky, 1961; Sutton & Barto, 2018). When an outcome produces a reward R, the learner must translate that scalar signal into evidence about decisions made earlier in the trajectory. This becomes particularly difficult when rewards are sparse and the actions responsible for them occurred hundreds or thousands of steps earlier. Reinforcement-learning algorithms commonly address temporal credit assignment using the discounted return

High-throughput simulators can partially compensate for this inefficiency by generating large numbers of trajectories. In real-world systems, however, interactions are expensive, delayed outcomes arrive slowly, and trajectories cannot be replayed freely. EWM complements conventional RL by replacing temporal proximity as the sole initial attribution prior. 

Conservative hierarchical operator (CHO) The EWM produces an explanation graph in which each phenomenon p is connected to a set of preceding events Cp = {c1, . . . , c K }, with attribution weights wp,c ∈ [0 , 1] . The redistribution operator uses this graph to determine where reward should appear in the trajectory. Unlike the fixed temporal coefficient γk, these weights depend on the realized outcome and the evidence in the trajectory. Reward is therefore assigned according to explanatory relevance rather than temporal proximity. The policy optimizer may still apply a discount factor after redistribution, but it operates on the reward stream produced by EWM. The redistribution is hierarchical because an event cited in one explanation may itself be a phe-nomenon with an explanation. Starting from a rewarded phenomenon, the operator divides its reward among its eligible predecessors and recursively repeats this process for each predecessor that can be explained. Recursion terminates when it reaches a maximum depth D, encounters an event without eligible predecessors, or the reward carried is below a defined threshold. The remaining reward is then deposited at that event. At each node, we retain only predecessors whose weight exceeds a threshold η, and normalize their weights locally:

Frontier models. We instantiate the EWM with a frontier language model, in our experiments 

Claude Sonnet 4.5 . Pretraining provides these models with broad priors about physical, procedural, and behavioral relations, while in-context reasoning allows them to apply that knowledge to previously unseen trajectories. We use the model as a backward-looking explainer rather than as a forward simulator, it observes both the completed trajectory and the realized phenomena, and identifies the preceding events that plausibly contributed to each phenomenon. Conveniently, a single model invocation produces the direct explanation graph for the entire trajectory. Then, the produced graph is used by CHO to redistribute reward programmatically to cited events recursively up to the selected depth. Recursion therefore requires no additional model calls, and the same cached explanation graph can be evaluated at different depths, or reward functions. 

EWM for RL. EWM integrates with reinforcement learning as an attribution layer between observing rewards and updating the policy. Given a completed or partial trajectory τ , we first obtain an explanation graph Hτ and then apply the conservative hierarchical operator R to produce a redistributed reward sequence: 

Hτ = EWM( τ ), ˜r = R(r, Hτ ). (3) The RL algorithm subsequently computes returns and advantages from ˜r rather than from the original reward sequence r. EWM therefore replaces a fixed notion of relevance based on temporal distance with trajectory-dependent attribution weights derived from the explanation graph. In the online setting, the policy interacts with the original environment, once an episode is complete or enough steps are gathered, EWM explains its realized outcomes and redistributes the rewards before value targets and policy gradients are computed. EWM does not otherwise modify the interaction protocol, policy architecture, or optimization algorithm, and can therefore be combined with other episodic RL methods that consume per-transition rewards. For offline learning, EWM is applied once to each logged trajectory. The resulting explanation graphs and redistributed rewards can be cached and used to train a policy with return-weighted or advantage-weighted objectives. 

4 LONG -H ORIZON RL E XPERIMENTS 

4.1 CRAFTER 

We first evaluate EWM in Crafter (Hafner, 2022), a controlled environment with long, hierarchical de-pendencies between actions and achievements. This setting allows us to isolate the credit-assignment problem and test whether explanation-based redistribution improves learning as rewards become increasingly delayed. We compare EWM against PPO and LaRe (Qu et al., 2025) baselines. We use the symbolic JAX implementation of Crafter (Matthews et al., 2024) and aggregate the rewards of the technology achievements into a single terminal reward given at the end of the episode. By doing this we can assess how delayed and long-horizon rewards pose a challenge to RL methods. We compare recurrent PPO and LaRe on this terminal reward stream with EWM at redistribution depth=1, with all methods using the same policy architecture and training configuration. On Crafter, Figure 3, vanilla PPO clearly cannot learn in delayed long-horizon environments like this. The gap between EWM and LaRe widens with L rather than with further training alone; at L=150 

the two credit methods are still close, while at L=1000 EWM is about 2× LaRe, consistent with the terminal reward becoming a longer-horizon assignment problem that redistribution is meant to solve. Moreover, in contrast to LaRe, EWM gets better with increases of L.4.2 WORD CRAFT 

We next evaluate EWM on WordCraft (Jiang et al., 2020), a combinatorial environment based on the Little Alchemy 2 game. Unlike Crafter’s largely fixed technology tree, WordCraft requires the agent to discover and compose recipes across a larger dependency graph, where early combinations may enable valuable discoveries several steps later. We aggregate all rewards at the end of the episode, creating a controlled long-horizon setting in which the agent must assign a sparse terminal signal 6Preprint

WordCraft therefore tests whether EWM can recover compositional dependencies beyond the sequential progression studied in Crafter. We evaluate a goal objective that provides a single reward when the target entity is created. 5 REAL -W ORLD EXPERIMENTS 

Our empirical study covers the KuaiRand dataset in three item 1 recommendation settings. Firstly, we use EWM in a reward-weighted fine-tuning of Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct, where we compare the baseline LaRe method and six EWM explanation-variants from Sec. 3. Then, we use the KuaiRand dataset in an online RL experiment, where we observe that EWM can accurately infer disengagement rules, thereby substantially improving cumulative return compared to vanilla methods like A2C (Mnih et al., 2016). Finally, we apply EWM to an industry-scale recommender .

6 CONCLUSION 

We introduced Explanatory World Models (EWM), which address long-horizon credit assignment by explaining realized outcomes and redistributing reward to relevant earlier events. Our conservative hierarchical operator redistributes credit to relevant events identified by the EWM. We instantiated EWM with a frontier language model and integrated it with both online and offline learning. Across Crafter and WordCraft, EWM outperforms PPO and LaRe under sparse rewards. It also improves over SFT and LaRe in offline KuaiRand experiments and over PPO online, while increasing recall and downstream engagement measures in an industry-scale generative recommender. These results show that explanations can provide structured credit when temporal propagation becomes inefficient. 9Preprint

B EXPERIMENT DETAILS 

B.1 KUAI RAND -OFFLINE 

We compare LaRe and six EWM-prompt-variants against a uniform-weight baseline. All experiments use the same Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct (Yang et al., 2024) model initialization, training recommendation samples, and training procedure. The experimental intervention is the weighting of supervised training examples derived from the assigned credit. 13 Preprint B.1.1 DATASET AND PREPROCESSING 

We use a preprocessed KuaiRand dataset containing chronological user-video interaction sequences. Binary engagement labels on the feed histories indicate whether an interaction includes a like, follow, forward, or long-view signal. The experiment uses a fixed subset of 1,000 user sessions from the preprocessed data. Following the stored session order, the first 700 sessions are used for training and the remaining 300 for evaluation. This is a fixed session-level partition, rather than a new random split for each training seed. Each evaluation session includes a held-out target from the randomly exposed interactions. Videos are represented through textual metadata, including available captions, creator information, and category descriptions. The implementation reuses a shared recommendation-data interface adapted for KuaiRand videos. B.1.2 RECOMMENDATION MODEL AND INPUT REPRESENTATION 

All experiments fine-tune the Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct model. Each supervised example contains the user’s preceding interaction history and a candidate video. The target response is either 

<answer>like</answer> or <answer>skip</answer> , according to the recorded binary engagement label. The credit-assignment method changes the weight of an example, while its input and target label remain unchanged. In other words, an explanation does not replace the observed engagement label with an LLM-generated label. The maximum input sequence length is 4,096 tokens. Training uses the model’s chat template, and the supervised loss is applied to answer tokens rather than to the history and candidate-description tokens. B.1.3 CREDIT -ASSIGNMENT MECHANISMS 

We compare eight methods: • Uniform, which assigns unweighted SFT. • LaRe, which uses the LaRe credit annotations to construct training weights. • B, a baseline EWM prompt. • HP, a prompt emphasizing Halpern-style counterfactual explanation. • D, a prompt emphasizing Deutsch-style explanation. • LB, a prompt emphasizing Lombrozo-style explanatory breadth. • LS, a prompt emphasizing Lombrozo-style explanatory simplicity. • LI, an integrated EWM prompt combining LB and LS. For the explanation-prompt variants, the annotation task concerns user retention. The annotator receives a chronological video history and an observed retention outcome, expressed through the user’s subsequent return activity. It identifies videos in the history that plausibly contributed to that outcome. The prompt requests strict JSON containing selected step indices, importance scores, and short explanations. At most five contributing steps are requested. Importance scores lie between zero and one and are not required to sum to one. B.2.1 DATASET AND SEQUENTIAL RECOMMENDATION ENVIRONMENT 

We use the KuaiRand environment provided by EasyRL4Rec (Yu et al., 2024), with is_click 

as the feedback target. Policy training uses the DeepFM user model to generate simulated feed-back. Evaluation uses the MF-based reward matrix. We follow the reward-model architecture and principal training configurations provided by EasyRL4Rec (Yu et al., 2024), using a five-member DeepFM ensemble for simulated training feedback and an MF-based rating matrix for policy evalu-ation (EasyRL4Rec/run; EasyRL4Rec/MF; EasyRL4Rec/utils.py). These scores are model-based surrogates for user feedback. An evaluation return therefore measures performance in the simulated recommendation environment rather than directly observed engagement from an online deployment. At the beginning of an episode, the environment samples a user and initializes the recommendation 15 Preprint state. The policy then repeatedly selects a video, receives feedback, and updates its state. Episodes end when the environment’s disengagement condition is met or when the maximum horizon of 30 recommendations is reached. The disengagement mechanism depends on repeated item tags within a history window. The configured parameter num_leave_compute is 10 and the leave threshold is zero. The oracle gate reproduces the implementation’s exact history-window construction. The environment and its termination mechanism remain the same across all four conditions. B.2.2 A2C POLICY 

The recommendation policy uses a shared two-layer network with hidden sizes 64 and 64, followed by an actor head and a value head. The actor produces a categorical distribution over candidate videos, and the critic estimates the return from the current state. The actor–critic and state tracker are optimized with separate Adam optimizers, both using learning rate 0.001. The discount factor is 0.9, the generalized advantage estimation parameter is 1.0, the value-loss coefficient is 0.5, and the entropy coefficient is zero. No explicit maximum gradient norm is configured in these runs. Training actions are sampled from the categorical policy. Evaluation uses the implementation’s deterministic action-selection mode. B.3 MOVIE LENS -1M In this section, we wish to answer the question: will EWM help in an online recommendation setting? Specifically, we study temporal credit assignment in a sequential recommendation setting using the MovieLens-1M dataset. The policy architecture, environment dynamics, and total reward are held fixed across all experimental conditions. The only experimental variable is how the reward of a completed recommendation session is distributed across individual recommendation actions before computing PPO returns and advantages. We compare four algorithms: dense ground truth rewards, terminal reward only, uniform redistribution, and EWM-based credit redistribution, which is an informed retrospective allocation of episode-level outcomes. B.3.1 DATASET AND PREPROCESSING 

We use the MovieLens-1M dataset, which contains one million explicit ratings from six thousand users across three thousand movies. The ratings lie on a five-point scale. We split the dataset using a global 90/10 split with random seed 42. This is neither a chronological split nor a per-user split, and the training set has nearly the same coverage as the validation set. 17 Preprint User features consist of user ID, gender, age group, and occupation. Movie features consist of movie ID and up to six genre fields. All categorical fields use eight-dimensional embeddings. The resulting concatenated user representation has 32 dimensions, while the concatenated movie representation has 56 dimensions. The environment uses dense zero-based user indices and movie indices. B.3.2 TRAINING AND EVALUATION REWARD MODELS 

The experiment uses two different dense reward matrices: • Five-model DeepFM ensemble, which is used to simulate online feedback • Six-factor matrix factorization, which is used only for policy evaluation and not for training, i.e. an independent surrogate evaluator 

DeepFM training simulator The training simulator is a five-member DeepFM ensemble. DeepFM predicts a rating from a static user–movie feature tuple and does not read the user’s sequential viewing history. For a given feature vector, its predicted mean combines a linear term, factorization-machine interactions, and a nonlinear network:

MF-based evaluation simulator Policy evaluation uses a separate matrix-factorization model with six-dimensional user and movie factors. Its predictions for all user-action pairs are clipped between 0 and 5 before being used by the environment. Consequently, an RL test return is a cumulative score from an MF-completed surrogate environment. 

What is the difference between DeepFM and MF? DeepFM and matrix factorization (MF) are fitted on the same 90% training split, but differ in their inputs, architectures, and optimization procedures. DeepFM uses user ID, gender, age group, and occupation, together with movie ID and genre features. Each categorical field has an eight-dimensional embedding. The model combines a linear component, factorization-machine interactions, and a nonlinear network with two 128-unit ReLU layers. Five DeepFM models are trained for five epochs each using Adam and batch size 2,048; their predictions are averaged to construct the dense training reward matrix. In contrast, MF uses only user and movie identities. It learns six-dimensional user and movie factors and predicts ratings through their inner products. The model is trained for 80 epochs using mean squared error and SGD with learning rate 0.1 and momentum 0.9. Its predictions form the dense evaluation reward matrix. Before use by the environment, the DeepFM ensemble predictions are shifted by subtracting their global minimum, whereas MF predictions are clipped to [0 , 5] . Optimizing dense DeepFM rewards does not necessarily maximize MF evaluation return. Since both models use the same interaction split, MF provides an alternative model-based evaluator rather than an independent source of ground-truth user feedback. B.3.3 SEQUENTIAL RECOMMENDATION ENVIRONMENT 

At the beginning of each episode, a user is sampled uniformly from all 6,040 users. The environment observation always consists of the user index and the most recent recommendation item. With random initialization enabled, the first observation contains a randomly initialized movie and zero feedback; this initial movie is not an action selected by the policy. The policy then acts by selecting one movie to recommend. Episodes have a maximum horizon of 30 actions. 18 Preprint B.3.4 ACTOR INPUT AND STATE REPRESENTATION 

Thus, the actor receives a 57-dimensional state containing the average movie representation and average recent feedback. The pretrained movie embeddings are initialized from the first DeepFM ensemble member. At the first action, the state consists of the randomly initialized movie embedding concatenated with zero feedback. B.3.5 PPO POLICY 

All algorithms use the same discrete PPO actor–critic network. The network has a shared two-layer MLP that maps the 57-dimensional state through hidden layers of sizes 64 and 64 with ReLU activation. Two separate heads of the network produce: (1) a masked categorical distribution over all actions; (2) a scalar estimate for the possible future returns expected from the current state. Training actions are sampled from the categorical policy, whereas evaluation uses the deterministic argmax action. Stochasticity during training comes from categorical sampling. Advantages are normalized within each minibatch. Gradients are clipped to a norm of 0.2. Both the actor–critic and state tracker are optimized with Adam at learning rate 0.001. B.3.6 TEMPORAL CREDIT -ASSIGNMENT CONDITIONS 

For a completed trajectory, we construct the PPO learning reward in four ways: • Dense, where the reward for each movie recommendation is passed to the PPO algorithm • Lump, where the algorithm only observes the terminal reward, i.e. the sum of all dense rewards • Uniform, where the terminal reward is distributed uniformly across all past recommendation actions • EWM, where the reward assigned to each step is inferred by the EWM framework B.3.7 EWM CREDIT REDISTRIBUTION 

In the current instantiation, EWM uses claude-sonnet-4-5 as a retrospective credit allocator. For each completed episode, the prompt contains three types of information: • First, a user profile is constructed exclusively from the training split, containing information such as gender, age group, occupation, etc. • Second, each recommended movie (i.e. past actions) is described using its title, genres, training-set average rating, and number of training raters. This is so that the EWM can have context on what the recommendation task looks like. • Third, EWM receives a scalar episode outcome Crucially, the individual step rewards are never included in the prompt to the EWM. The EWM sees only the aggregate terminal reward. The EWM must return strict JSON, assigning the terminal reward to every recommended episode. Invalid or repeatedly unsuccessful judgments do not zero the trajectory; instead, the original Lump allocation is retained. Successful judgments are stored in an append-only cache keyed by the prompt version, user, and exact ordered movie sequence. RL training runs share this cache and are run sequentially. This improves efficiency and ensures that an identical cached trajectory receives the same credit allocation, and also reduces the noise from LLM judgment. The current configuration uses eight concurrent requests, a limit of 100 attempts per minute, a 240-second timeout, up to 2,400 output tokens, and a budget of 45,000 fresh episodes per seed. Two API keys alternate requests to the same model and endpoint; they are a throughput mechanism, not two independent judges. Temperature is not explicitly specified. B.3.8 TRAINING BUDGET 

We use seeds 2023, 2024, and 2025. Each condition is trained for 100 epochs and 100 episodes per epoch. PPO uses batch size 1,024 and one optimization pass per minibatch. 19 Preprint B.3.9 EVALUATION PROTOCOL 

Evaluation uses the MF-based environment and deterministic action selection. One evaluation is performed before training and one after every epoch. Each evaluation contains 100 episodes. Additional metrics include episode length, mean reward per turn, catalog coverage, intra-session diversity, and novelty.
