# Federated Semantic Residuals for Multimodal Recommendation 

Zhiwei Li , Guodong Long , Chengqi Zhang , Fellow, IEEE , and Jing Jiang 

Abstract —Federated Recommendation Systems (FedRSs) learn collaborative scores from interaction histories on device and rank items by them, so a higher score means a higher rank. Item text and images add semantic information that can reorder the list. Existing Federated Multimodal Recommenders (FedMRs) fuse this content into their models, which can obscure how content reorders the list. We therefore propose the SEmantic Residual Adapter (SERA), which keeps content out of an existing FedRS and adds semantic residual scores to its collaborative scores. Content can then lift an item above another only when its semantic residual score exceeds the other’s by more than their collaborative score gap. This score grows with the item’s content similarity to the user’s interaction history and is capped at a residual bound. We prove that for any content, an item can enter the list only if its collaborative score is within twice this bound of the lowest one in the list. A larger bound lets content lift more items, including irrelevant ones, so the bound trades off how far content reaches against accuracy. With a validated bound, SERA improves all three FedRSs on all six datasets without adding personalized parameters. It outperforms FedMRs in most comparisons and lifts some FedRSs above centralized baselines. 

Index Terms —Federated Recommendation System, Multi-modal Recommendation System, On-Device Learning 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Federated Recommendation Systems (FedRSs) learn collab-orative scores from interaction histories stored on user devices [1] and rank items by these scores, so an item appears above another if it scores higher. Observed interactions provide the main supervision [2], [3], and each user or item embedding is learned from the gradients of its own interactions [4]. As a result, users and items with sparse histories have too few interactions to learn reliable ID-based scores [5], [6]. Item text and images ease this sparsity, because they exist before an item collects many interactions [7]. They add seman-tic information to interaction histories [8] and help multimodal recommenders refine collaborative rankings [9], [10]. A. Federated Recommendation Systems 

FedRSs learn from interaction histories stored on user devices by aggregating client updates [17], [18]. Federated Collaborative Filtering keeps user factors local and aggregates item-factor gradients to learn shared item representations [2]. FedNCF extends this design to neural models and aggregates sparse item embeddings separately from dense neural param-eters [19]. Differences in client interaction distributions moti-vate personalized models [20], [21]. PFedRec retains a local user model and adapts shared item representations to local interactions [22]. FedRAP decomposes item representations into globally shared and user-specific components [4], while GPFedRec builds a user-relation graph from client-specific item embeddings for personalized aggregation [23]. To ac-commodate device resources, HeteFedRec transfers knowledge across models with different embedding dimensions through dual-task learning and relation-based distillation [24]. These FedRSs provide the collaborative scores that SERA builds on. 

B. Multimodal Recommendation 

Multimodal recommenders supplement interaction supervi-sion with item text and images [8], [25]. VBPR projects visual features into a user-specific visual preference term added to collaborative scores [26], while ACF uses item and component attention to aggregate multimodal interaction histories [27]. In item-based collaborative filtering, NAIS weights history items by relevance to the candidate [28]. SERA weights history content by collaborative scores to form one query for all candidates. MMCN refines item representations through attention-based residuals and hierarchical contrastive learning [29]. MICRO learns modality-aware item graphs and combines shared and modality-specific signals through contrastive learn-ing [30], while FREEDOM freezes content-derived graphs and denoises interaction graphs [31]. BM3 combines interaction reconstruction with cross-modal alignment [32], and MMSSL contrasts collaborative and multimodal views [33]. Dropout-Net reconstructs cold-item representations from content [34], while CLCRec aligns content and collaborative spaces [35]. PCKD reduces preference inconsistencies across distillation projection through a ranking regularization term [36]. Content reach also relates to studies that limit changes to existing lists. Additive corrections to a frozen ranker and score regularization trade accuracy gains against the share of queries whose rankings change [37]. CertDR uses randomized smooth-ing to certify top-K exclusion under word-substitution attacks [38]. Reachability analyses ask whether a user can make an item recommended by changing their own interactions [39], [40]. SERA instead bounds the collaborative score gaps that semantic residual scores can overcome for any content. 

C. Federated Multimodal Recommendation 

FedMRs integrate item content under federated resource and privacy constraints [41]. Their designs differ in fusion level and in the item representations that clients and servers ex-change. IFedRec learns an attribute network from aggregated item embeddings and aligns its output with local collaborative representations to serve cold-start items [6]. AMMFRS fuses text, image, identity, and behavior features through client-side attention [11]. FedMMR aligns a server-side multimodal model with lightweight collaborative models on clients [15]. FedVLR generates vision-language views on the server and combines them through a client-specific mixture of experts [13]. GFMFR uses server-side group-level multimodal repre-sentations as distillation targets for local training [16]. SERA instead fuses content at the score level and personalizes its semantic residual scores with local interaction histories. III. P RELIMINARIES

A. Federated Optimization 

Algorithm 1 splits the optimization of Eq. (12) between clients and the server, which holds the item features and the projector hϕ. At round t, the server computes the content table Zt = [zi]i∈I by Eq. (4), samples clients St, and normalizes their data counts into weights αu,t that sum to one. Each sampled client receives Zt with the shared collaborative parameters, performs E local steps of the FedRS, and keeps its personalized parameters on the device. Over these updates, it accumulates the gradient Gtu of its residual loss with respect to Zt and uploads it with its collaborative model update. The server applies the FedRS’s aggregation rule, averages the uploaded gradients with weights αu,t , and back-propagates the average to update hϕ. Every local step reads the table built from ϕt. For fixed collaborative parameters, the update thus follows an unbiased gradient of Eq. (11) with αu replaced by E[αu,t ]. If this objective is smooth with bounded gradient variance and the step size scales as T −1/2, standard nonconvex analysis [43] shows that its average squared gradient norm is 

O(T −1/2). Algorithm 1 computes σu from all M collaborative scores. At inference, the client builds its query from its history and the stored table and ranks the candidate set Cu by s(u, i ).

For κ above all positive change points of i, its final rank is its rank by similarity cui , and collaborative order breaks ties. Proof Sketch. The items above i in the final ranking are those with higher collaborative scores that i has not passed and those with lower ones that have passed it. By Lemma 1, each of these pairs changes ranking only at a positive change point. By Proposition 2, an item j with ρu(j) > K lies in the final top-K list exactly when Pj − Qj ≥ ρu(j) − K, which refines the reach condition of Theorem 1 into an exact test. An item can also rise and later fall. Each positive change point with a higher competitor raises the item by one position, and each one with a lower competitor lowers it by one, so an outside item can enter the top-K list and later leave it.

E. Privacy Discussion 

SERA keeps user interaction histories Hu and personalized parameters θu on the client, where each client builds its content query qu, computes the semantic residual scores r(u, i ), and ranks items by s(u, i ). B. Baselines 

The baselines serve three roles. Centralized multimodal recommenders show the accuracy when a model learns from interactions and item content in a centralized setting.  

> •

VBPR [26] extends BPR with pretrained visual item fea-tures for visually informed user preference estimation.  

> •

BM3 [32] learns multimodal representations through self-supervised reconstruction without negative sampling.  

> •

MGCN [50] denoises modality features and propagates them on modality-specific graphs for behavior-aware fusion.  

> •

FITMM [51] decomposes multimodal representations into frequency bands and regularizes their information content.  

> •

PGL [52] extracts principal user-item subgraphs to preserve local interaction structure during graph message passing.  

> •

MSCA [53] aligns collaborative and modality-augmented views through semantic contrastive representation learning. 

ID-based FedRSs provide the backbones for measuring the effect of adding multimodal item content.  

> •

FedAvg [17] aggregates shared model parameters through client-weighted averaging across participating clients.  

> •

PFedRec [22] learns lightweight client models with locally personalized user and item representations on the device.  

> •

FedRAP [4] decomposes item representations into shared and personalized components for federated personalization. 

FedMRs provide federated multimodal alternatives that add item content at the feature, representation, or objective level.  

> •

FedMMR [15] aligns client collaborative and server multi-modal item representations through a contrastive objective.  

> •

AMMFRS [11] fuses text, image, identity, and behavior fea-tures through client-side attention before federated training.  

> •

FedVLR [13] constructs server-side multimodal views and selects them through a client-specific mixture of experts.  

> •

GFMFR [16] distills group-level multimodal preferences into each client’s own local recommendation objective. We attach SERA to the same FedAvg, PFedRec, and FedRAP backbones that FedVLR and GFMFR extend. All methods use the same framework, data splits, and evaluation protocols.

D. Parameter Analysis 

Table IV shows that SERA adds no personalized or client-side trainable parameters on any backbone or dataset. When validation selects a multilayer projector, SERA’s server-side projector has up to 410K parameters. E. Content and Personalized Ranking 1) Content and Modality Controls: Fig. 4 compares each full model with SERA retrained on altered content. Shuffling permutes features across items, and the random-feature control replaces them with random vectors of the same mean and variance. Both controls usually reduce mean accuracy. Image removal causes more consistent losses than text removal. On Cards, image removal lowers mean accuracy with every backbone, and text removal raises it for FedAvg and PFedRec. The shuffled and random-feature controls detach each item from its own features, so the query in Eq. (6) and the similarity in Eq. (7) stop reflecting the content of the user’s history. The modalities also contribute unequally to zi in Eq. (4). On Cards and Food, the image features vary more across items than the text features, and many Cards items share nearly identical title features. Removing images discards the more variable modality, while removing text retains it and produces smaller gains or losses in ranking accuracy across backbones. 

2) Residual and Content Contributions: Fig. 5 compares SERA with separately trained zero-residual and shuffled-content controls. The zero-residual control uses collaborative scores alone. Shuffling keeps the residual computation un-changed, so this control tests whether each item needs its own features. SERA achieves positive mean gains with FedRAP over both controls on every displayed dataset, while some gains with FedAvg and PFedRec are near zero or negative. 

3) User-Level Ranking Changes: Fig. 6 holds each trained model fixed and compares users’ NDCG@50 with and without its semantic residual scores. More users improve than decline. An item overtakes a competitor when their residual difference exceeds their collaborative score gap in Eqs. (3) and (13). Pairwise ranking changes can improve accuracy by promoting the test item within the top 50 or into it. By Corollary 2, NDCG@50 stays fixed for a user when the test item has only one possible final rank or can only rank below the top 50. 

F. Shared Projector Learning 1) Bias and Weight Learning: Fig. 7 measures the gain of bias learning over a fixed projector and the added gain of weight learning. Bias learning usually improves accuracy. Weight learning often lowers it, except for FedRAP on Cards. The frozen features lie in a narrow cone [54], and a fixed projector in Eq. (4) keeps all items close to their mean direction. Every item’s residual gradient updates the same bias vector, so the shared biases can remove this common direction and spread the representations apart. The weights far outnum-ber the biases and must fit the sparse feedback in Table I, so their updates rarely improve on the bias shift. Bias-only tuning likewise rivals full tuning of pretrained transformers on small data [55]. Eqs. (6) and (7) use these representations, so bias updates change all users’ semantic residual scores at once. 

2) Feature Centering: Fig. 8 tests whether subtracting the mean item feature reduces SERA’s gain from bias learning. Centering removes most of this gain on Food, where SERA uses a linear projector. On Beauty and Cards, most of the gain remains in SERA’s multilayer projectors, whose hidden-layer biases continue to adjust the content representations after input centering, altering both queries and candidate similarities. SERA fuses item content with a FedRS at the score level, adding semantic residual scores that are learned from local histories and capped at a user-adaptive residual bound. Each rank change is then a pairwise ranking change in which a residual difference overcomes a collaborative score gap, and each pair changes ranking at most once as the budget grows. For any content, an item outside the collaborative top-K list can enter the top K only if its collaborative boundary gap is at most twice the residual bound. Larger budgets let content cross wider gaps but can lower accuracy by moving competitors above the test items. SERA improves all three FedRSs on all six datasets without adding personalized parameters. A. Training and Evaluation 

Item features and the federated training setup follow Fed-VLR [13]. A pretrained CLIP ViT-B/32 model [56] extracts frozen 512-dimensional text and image features, and the con-tent representations have 64 dimensions. All clients participate in every round, and each client runs E = 5 local steps with a batch size of 2,048. Unless specified otherwise below, training stops after 10 consecutive rounds without improvement in validation NDCG@50 or after 100 rounds, and testing uses the best validation round. The runs in Fig. 14 instead use a fixed number of rounds T without early stopping or checkpoint selection and report the mean test NDCG@50 over rounds 

T − 3 to T . Ranking over I masks each user’s training and validation items. 

B. Model Selection

For FedAvg and PFedRec on ML, the 10 best hyperparameter settings are each trained with five seeds, and the one with the highest mean validation NDCG@50 is kept. The search uses seed 2024. All reported results except Fig. 8 use seeds 2025 to 2029, and the reselection on ML uses only their validation scores. Table VI lists the selected hyperparameter values.

SERA’s semantic residual score has three design compo-nents: the user-adaptive residual scale in Eq. (9), the stop-gradient in Eq. (3), and the history weights in Eq. (5). To test each one, we swap it for a simpler alternative and keep the rest of SERA fixed. The shared scale gives every user the same scale ¯σ, the mean of σu over the N users of the trained model. Joint training removes the stop-gradient on the collaborative score, so the residual loss also updates the backbone, while 

βui and σu keep their stop-gradient. Uniform weighting is approximated by multiplying τq by 10 6.

a) User-Adaptive Residual Scale: SERA scales each user’s semantic residual scores by σu, the standard deviation of that user’s collaborative scores over the item set. Larger variation in collaborative scores permits larger semantic resid-ual magnitudes. By Proposition 1 and Theorem 1, one budget 

κ then gives every user a reach band of equal width in units of 

σu. Under the shared scale, these widths differ across users. Replacing σu by ¯σ scales user u’s semantic residual scores by the budget ratio ¯σ/σ u, so users with σu below ¯σ get a wider reach band than under SERA and those above it a narrower one. Table VIII shows two consistent differences that keep their sign in every seed. Both appear on Cards and point in opposite directions: compared with SERA, the shared scale raises Recall@50 for FedAvg and lowers it for FedRAP. The budget sweeps on Cards in Figs. 10 and 11 show a similar pattern. Above the trained budget, SERA on FedAvg keeps gaining accuracy, whereas SERA on FedRAP loses accuracy as its harmed share of users grows fastest. The shared scale raises the budget ratio of users with small σu, so for them it acts like a larger budget. On Cards, the shared scale helps SERA on backbones that benefit from larger budgets and hurts SERA on the backbone that suffers from them. SERA on PFedRec follows the same pattern on Cards. b) Stop-Gradient: SERA’s stop-gradient in Eq. (3) passes the collaborative score forward and blocks its gradient, and SERA also applies the stop-gradient to the collaborative scores used for the history weights and σu. Consequently, the residual loss in Eq. (11) trains only the projector, and the backbone keeps learning from Lbase (a) alone. All of SERA’s content correction therefore sits in the semantic residual score r(u, i ),which Proposition 1, Corollary 1, and Theorem 1 bound relative to the trained collaborative scores. Joint training opens a path from the residual loss to the backbone through s(u, i ),and this path changes what the backbone learns. As Sec. VI-G notes, the pairwise loss in Eq. (1) becomes less sensitive to score changes as the margin between the observed item and the sampled negative grows. Under joint training this margin includes r(u, i ), so pairs that r(u, i ) already ranks correctly send weaker gradients to the backbone and pairs that it ranks wrongly send stronger ones. The backbone then learns from errors that already include the semantic residual score, so its collaborative scores carry content information, and the bounds of Sec. V cover only the content effect left in r(u, i ).Table VIII supports this choice. Its two consistent differ-ences, both for PFedRec on Food, favor SERA’s stop-gradient. Every other difference changes sign across seeds, including joint training’s largest mean advantage, for PFedRec on Cards. The stop-gradient thus keeps SERA’s guarantees in Sec. V-B intact and matches joint training within seed variation. 

c) History Weights: SERA builds its content query from the user’s history and weights each history item by its collab-orative score in Eq. (5). The temperature τq sets how sharply these weights favor high-scoring items, and a larger τq spreads them more evenly. Uniform weighting is the large-τq limit. In Table VI, validation picks one of the two largest τq values in five of the nine configurations. How much the weights matter depends on the history itself. The content query in Eq. (6) is a normalized weighted average of the representations zi of the history items, so the weights can only mix the directions of these items into qu. When the items point in similar directions, any weighting gives nearly the same query, and Eqs. (7) to (9) then give nearly the same semantic residual scores. The history weights therefore matter mainly for users whose histories mix dissimilar content. In Table VIII, every difference between SERA’s history weights and uniform weighting changes sign across seeds, including FedAvg on Food, where SERA’s validated τq is the grid’s smallest. This content table also explains how SERA compares with the other adapters. Compared with GFMFR, which distills server-side multimodal content into each client’s backbone using group preference targets, SERA also exchanges its content table. In return, SERA keeps content in r(u, i ), which leaves the backbone’s training unchanged. FedVLR uses the same collaborative item table as SERA, so their communication differs by SERA’s M × d content table and FedVLR’s fusion network of about 1.24 ×10 5 parameters. Consequently, SERA exchanges less than FedVLR on the four datasets with fewer than about 1.9×10 3 items and more on ML and Movie. SERA stores d-dimensional rows, a sixteenth of the width of the dx-dimensional features that FedVLR stores, so SERA needs far less on-device storage. The server-side projector also keeps SERA’s GPU memory low. Because the projector’s parameters, optimizer states, and dx-dimensional activations stay on the server, SERA’s peak GPU memory stays close to that of a backbone of the same width. SERA’s added tensors, namely the content table, its gradient, and the collaborative scores over the item set, all grow with M , so its peak GPU memory on Dance, Food, and Movie rises roughly in proportion to the number of items. At each history position, SERA keeps a d-dimensional row, whereas FedVLR keeps dx-dimensional features and fusion outputs. The long histories of ML multiply this gap, so SERA’s peak GPU memory on ML is a small fraction of FedVLR’s. 

b) Time: SERA’s time cost follows from the size of its operations. Its client batches hold one user, and its server projects at most a few thousand items per round. At these sizes, fixed kernel launch overhead sets the duration of each GPU operation. SERA’s added time therefore depends on how many operations it adds and changes little with the embedding ARXIV PREPRINT 17 

width or the number of items. Table X confirms this: outside ML, SERA’s client computation stays nearly constant across item sets differing in size by over an order of magnitude. Operation counts also explain how SERA compares with the other methods at each stage. In local training, SERA adds the parameter-free steps of Eqs. (5) to (9) to its backbone. These steps need fewer operations than FedVLR’s fusion net-work, so SERA’s client computation is lower than FedVLR’s outside ML. On the server, SERA runs one forward pass, one backward pass, and one optimizer step per round for all clients, so its server stage is on the same order of magnitude as FedMMR’s and more than two orders of magnitude below GFMFR’s clustering and fusion rounds. At inference, SERA computes all similarities of a user with one matrix-vector product between the content table and qu. This is more work than GFMFR, whose inference uses the backbone alone, and less than FedVLR, whose inference runs its fusion network, so SERA’s time per user lies between theirs everywhere. 

c) Exception on ML: ML departs from this pattern because two effects raise SERA’s client computation there. First, the data loading and negative sampling that SERA shares with its backbone grow with each user’s interactions, which are more than an order of magnitude more numerous on ML. Second, SERA pads each history with item 0 to the longest one, and in the backward pass of its table lookup, positions that share a row add their gradients one at a time. Since the longest ML history is an order of magnitude longer than elsewhere, most positions of an ML history are padding and write in turn into the row of item 0. Profiling confirms this second effect. The accumulation takes about 4.6 ms per ML client with SERA’s server-side projector and about 0.3 ms with a client-side projector. Reading the table with index_select 

instead cuts SERA’s client computation on ML by 17% and changes it on Beauty by about 3%, so the serialized accumulation matters only when user histories are long. 

d) Equal-Width Comparison: The backbones in Table X use their own tuned hyperparameters, so the extra time of each SERA row mixes SERA’s cost with differences in hy-perparameters. To measure SERA’s own cost, we also time SERA against a backbone of the same 64-dimensional width on Beauty, Cards, and Food. With its server-side projector, SERA needs 1.8 to 2.0 times the client computation of this backbone and 1.1 to 1.9 times its inference time per user, and SERA’s peak GPU memory stays within 0.3 MiB of the backbone’s.
