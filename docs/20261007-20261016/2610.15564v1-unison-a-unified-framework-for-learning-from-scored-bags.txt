# Abstract 

We introduce UNISON, a unified framework that bridges multiple instance learn-ing and recommendation systems by treating both as instances of learning from scored bag collections of (item, score) pairs. Unlike traditional approaches that address bag-level classification and personalized recommendation as separate problems, UNISON decomposes learning into three interconnected stages: (1) learning universal item representations, (2) modeling bag-specific preference pat-terns through lightweight personalized parameters, and (3) inferring bag-level characteristics from learned preferences. We justify the architectural choice of linear bag-specific scoring through a first-order analysis: when item embeddings are sufficiently smooth in the score, a linear function approximates the score up to a Taylor remainder controlled by the curvature of the underlying preference function. For cold-start scenarios, we show that the cold-bag fitting problem reduces to ridge-less linear regression with a closed-form solution, and provide convergence guarantees for the iterative variant. Empirically, UNISON matches or outperforms state-of-the-art approaches across diverse domains: entertainment (books, movies), immunology (T cell receptor (TCR) based classification), and microbiome, offering superior computational efficiency and interpretability. A controlled synthetic experiment further confirms that the bag-specific parameters (wj , b j ) recover the latent factors that drive item scores even when the projection 

ϕ has no access to the original scoring mechanism (AUC > 0.998). UNISON is available at https://github.com/louzounlab/UNISON. 

# 1 Introduction 

Consider seemingly disparate problems: (1) predicting the condition of a host based on its RNA expression level, (2) recommending books or movies to a reader based on their history of (book, rating) pairs, (3) predicting the frequency of a microbe in the gut of a host, and propose a new movie for a viewer to see. Despite arising in different domains, all these problems and many more share a fundamental structure: learning from scored bags , collections of items where each item is associated with a quantitative value. In Biology, the bag is the host and the items are genes or microbes scored by their presence/absence or their frequency. In books or movies, the score is their rating by the reader/viewer. Traditional multiple instance learning (MIL) [Dietterich et al., 1997] addresses the problem of learning from bags when only bag-level labels are available, treating all instances as equally weighted. Conversely, recommendation systems [Koren et al., 2009] focus on predicting scores for new items given a user’s interaction history (in the current context, the users’ scores for some known items), typically relying on auxiliary features or meta-learning for cold-start scenarios [Lee et al., 2019] (cold start is the prediction for previously unknown users or items). We argue that both problems are instances of the same fundamental task and can be addressed within a unified framework. Specifically, we argue that the relation between items and their scores are determined by bag-specific latent arXiv:2610.15564v1 [cs.LG] 11 Oct 2026 variables, and that these variables can be used to classify the entire bag. Moreover, we propose that by having the shared training produce an embedding that can be linearly associated with the score (or with a function of the score) of each item. 

1.1 Related Work and Novelty 

UNISON bridges three fields: MIL, recommendation systems, and "learn to rank". 

Multiple Instance Learning: Early MIL work [Dietterich et al., 1997, Maron and Lozano-Pérez, 1997] focused on witness identification. Modern approaches use attention mechanisms [Ilse et al., 2018] or transformers [Shao et al., 2021], but treat instance weights as implicit latent variables. UNISON extends MIL by incorporating explicit instance scores. 

Recommendation Systems: Classical methods include collaborative filtering [Sarwar et al., 2001] and content-based approaches [Mooney and Roy, 2000]. Neural collaborative filtering (NCF) [He et al., 2017] extends matrix factorization with neural networks to model complex user-item interac-tions, while graph-based methods like LightGCN [He et al., 2020] leverage user-item bipartite graphs through simplified graph convolutions. EASE [Steck, 2019] demonstrates that linear models with closed-form solutions can achieve competitive performance through proper regularization. Hybrid methods [Burke, 2002] combine both paradigms. Recent meta-learning approaches like MeLU [Lee et al., 2019] and MAMO [Dong et al., 2020] address cold-start through expensive gradient-based adaptation, while ColdNAS [Wu et al., 2023] uses neural architecture search for user-specific models. UNISON moves most of the training to an item embedding, ensuring that the scores in each bag can be learnt through a linear classifier, leading to superior cold start performance with lightweight linear optimization, requiring only interaction data without user features. 

Learn-to-Rank: L2R methods [Liu, 2009] learn ranking functions from preference data. UNISON extends this by framing item level ranking and bag-level classification as combined optimization objectives, and separate the training into a multi-bag complex embedding, and a per-bag linear classifier that can be trained using a small number of entity-score pairs per bag. Within this context, we make the following theoretical and practical novel contributions: 1. Unified Framework: We formalize the scored bag learning problem and show that bag classification and item scoring are dual objectives that can be trained within the same framework. More specifically, we propose simultaneously training a bag-specific weight vector and a combined item embedding whose product is the score assigned in this bag to the item, and the weights are the latent representation of the bag. We empirically show that this representation is highly informative and leads to high-accuracy bag classification. Weights were used in meta-learning to predict network accuracy Finn et al. [2017]. We show here that the same concept can be adapted to bag classification. 2. Theoretical Analysis: We provide a first-order Taylor analysis showing that linear bag-specific scoring is consistent with any sufficiently smooth preference function up to a curvature-dependent error (Theorem 1), and we characterize the convergence behavior of the cold-start optimization (Proposition 1). The latter reduces to ridgeless linear regression and admits a closed-form solution. 3. Efficient Cold-Start: We use a low dimension for the embedding and the appropriate weights, leading to minimal inference-time optimization. Because the cold-start objective is a convex quadratic in ( wj , b j ), it can be solved rapidly in closed form. This low-dimensional product outperforms gradient-based meta-learning approaches both theoretically and empiri-cally. 4. Domain Generality: We validate UNISON across six diverse datasets spanning entertain-ment, immunology, and ecology, showing consistent performance with minimal domain-specific engineering. 5. Identifiability of Functional Embeddings: We provide a controlled experiment in which item scores are generated by a known group-specific weight, and a different embedder is used for training. UNISON nevertheless recovers the group label with AUC >0.998, demonstrating that

# 5 Results 

5.1 Toy Model validation of concept 

Stage 3 uses the learned preference parameters (wj , b j ) as functional embeddings for bag classifica-tion. This succeeds when the bag level label yj is governed by the same latent factors that determine the item-score relationship within the bag. To verify that the informativeness of (wj , b j ) for bag classification stems from its structural role as a preference encoding rather than from dimensionality reduction by ϕ, we conducted a controlled sanity check. We partitioned Book Crossing users into two groups (A and B) and generated synthetic item scores using embedder A, multiplying each item embedding by a group-specific weight vector ( Wa for group A, Wb for group B). We then trained UNISON using a completely different embedder B, ensuring that ϕ has no direct access to the original scoring mechanism. UNISON achieved AUC = 0 .9989 on group classification, confirming that (wj , b j ) recovers the latent structure that drives item scores, independent of any artifact of the projection. 

5.2 UNISON application to book scoring 

To evaluate UNISON, we analyzed a standard item ranking task from the Book-Crossing dataset Ziegler et al. [2005]. In this task, one is expected to compute the score a new user would give to a previously unseen book. In the UNISON language, the book is the item, the score is the score, and the user is the bag. Books are embedded using frozen Qwen3-8B encoders to obtain zk (stage 1). They are then projected through ϕ to yield ˜zk. Then user-specific parameters (wj , b j ) from a support set of k rated books are learned. These personalized parameters are used to predict ratings on query books (stage 2). Finally, the same personalized parameters are used to predict user age group (above or below 32 years - stage 3) (Fig. 1A). We first evaluated the accuracy of stage 2 on the entire test set of new users simultaneously - i.e., compare ˆsk,j and sk,j for all j and k (Fig. 1B). The Spearman correlation coefficient (SCC) obtained is SCC = 0 .462 , p < 1.e − 3. One could argue that this correlation represents the difference between bags (users), rather than the difference between the scores the same user gave different books. To test for that, we computed the Spearman correlation between ˆsk,j and sk,j for each user separately (Fig. 1C). One can see that for over 90 % of the users, there is a positive correlation ( p < 1.e − 3 t-test on the SCC of each user). As mentioned, we assume that even in the test set, we have a subset of books with known scores. To ensure that such a subset can be defined, we analyzed the distribution of ratings per user (Fig. 1D). While the dataset is inherently sparse, a significant subset of users provided enough interactions to support inference. We thus focused our analysis on users with at least 10 ratings and tested the accuracy of stages 2 and 3 as a function of the support set size (Fig. 1E). Increasing the support set size improves the regression, but limits the number of users, and as such, can reduce the accuracy of stage 3. Indeed, both stages reach a maximal prediction accuracy (SCC, AUC) at a support size of 40. To compare to a naive cosine distance on the original embedding and test whether the transition from zk to ˜zk is necessary, we computed the accuracy of stages 2 and 3, with and without this 7stage (Fig. 1F), and indeed the accuracy in both stages is much higher when ˜zk is learned within UNISON. Finally, given the different stages, we tested whether each stage can be trained separately, or whether all stages should be trained simultaneously (Fig. 1G), and no significant difference was found. Note that, as mentioned above, to keep UNISON generic, we assume that stage I was trained on a domain-specific task. 

5.3 Simple Inference-Time Learning Is Sufficient 

To evaluate UNISON’s generalization, we partitioned the dataset into four scenarios based on UNISON’s familiarity with users and items during training, as described above (WU-WI, WU-CI, CU-WI, and CU-CI). For each user, we define a Support Set consisting of Nsup items labeled as Warm, to avoid any possible leakage by assuming known scores for cold items. The Query Set contains the remaining items: any additional Warm items beyond Nsup , as well as all items labeled as Cold that the user has rated (Fig. 2A). When faced with the challenge of "Cold-Start" - where we must infer preferences for a completely new bag from only a few scored items - one might assume that heavy, gradient-based meta-learning frameworks are necessary (e.g. MeLU Lee et al. [2019]). We argue instead that if the shared item representations ( ˜zk) are sufficiently informative, a simple linear mapping (wj , b j ) is not only sufficient. To test this, we compared UNISON against gradient-based meta-learning across several benchmark scenarios. In the MovieLens dataset, specifically the most difficult Cold-User Cold-Item (CU-CI) setting, UNISON achieves a Spearman correlation of 

0.323 , outperforming the far more computationally expensive MeLU ( 0.291 , p < 0.01 , two-sided permutation test with 10,000 permutations; Fig. 2B). The advantage of this minimalist approach becomes even more striking in the Book-Crossing dataset; here, UNISON reaches a correlation of 0.4736 , while MeLU fails to effectively generalize, yielding only 0.1114 (p < 0.001 , Fig. 2B). These results suggest a fundamental trade-off: while complex models have more "capacity" to learn, they are also more prone to overfitting when the support set is sparse. To evaluate this overfitting in MeLU’s adaptation mechanism, we compared query set loss to support set loss across 723 cold users. A paired-samples t-test revealed that query loss ( M = 1 .29 , SD = 0 .80 ) was significantly higher than support loss ( M = 0 .78 , SD = 0 .46 ), t-test, p < . 001 , Cohen’s d = 0 .62 , demonstrating substantial overfitting during few-shot adaptation. In contrast, UNISON’s performance remains stable and does not show any over-fitting (Fig. 2B,C). This design choice reflects a deliberate trade-off: by optimizing UNISON for the most challenging double cold-start scenario (CU-CI), we reduce the dimension of ˜z, and sacrifice some performance in partially warm settings. In MovieLens, while UNISON excels at CU-CI ( 0.323 vs. 0.291 ), MeLU performs better when either users or items are warm: WU-CI ( 0.476 vs. 0.377 ) and CU-WI ( 0.301 

vs. 0.260 ) (Fig. 2B, C). However, Book-Crossing shows UNISON outperforming across all scenarios (Fig. 2B, C). The distribution of user items in MovieLens follows a similar long-tail pattern to Book-Crossing. For k = 40 , we achieved SCC = 0 .323 and AU C = 0 .675 (Fig. 3 left panel). While the performance gap when transitioning from zk to ˜zk was narrower than in Book-Crossing, the ablation study demonstrates its importance: without this stage, performance drops to SCC = 0 .299 

and AU C = 0 .663 (p < 0.01 , paired t-test). 

5.4 Framework Generalizes Cross Domain 

To demonstrate that UNISON extends beyond entertainment, we applied it to two biological domains: TCR-peptide binding (immunology) and microbiome-disease associations (ecology). The architecture of Stage II (preference learning) and Stage III (bag classification) remains unchanged across domains; only the initial item encoder (Stage I) is domain-specific. We examine two complementary challenges. In the Microbiome cohorts , we used three microbiome samples, where the samples were divided to control and disease cases (see Appendix). We then tested the AUC for predicting microbe frequencies (Fig. 3 right panel), and the AUC for host condition classification. The prediction of microbe frequencies from their embedding is a new task that has never been applied to the microbiome. The AUC obtained here is higher than in the original publication for the same tasks (AUC 0.68 vs. 0.63 Shtossel et al. [2023]). In the TCR-peptide interactions , we analyze the bulk sequencing of TCRs and predict the presence/absence of TCR clones within each repertoire (Stage 2 - with binary classification) and classify the CMV serostatus of the host (Stage 3). In the CU-CI scenario, performance remains stable across support sizes, reaching Spearman 0.37 (Stage 2) and AUC 0.64 (Stage 3) at k = 3500 , where the large bag sizes ( 10 5 clonotypes per repertoire) make the model less sensitive to support set variations (Fig. 3 central panel). # 6 Discussion 

Our results validate several theoretical predictions: 

Linear scoring is consistent with smooth preferences: The strong empirical performance with linear scoring confirms the first-order analysis of Theorem 1. When ϕ learns discriminative representations, non-linear scoring adds minimal value while increasing overfitting risk on small support sets. 

Generalization: Bag classification performance of 0.62 − 0.70 AUC using only functional embed-dings (wj , b j ) across biological cohorts, and AUC > 0.998 in the controlled synthetic identifiability experiment, supports our proposition about the association between (wj , b j ) and the bag class. 9Figure 3: Effect of support set size on UNISON performance across three domains: TCR (left), and Microbiome (right). For MovieLens and TCR, we report both SCC (green) and AUC (orange). For Microbiome, we compare Stage 2 (solid, light) vs Stage 3 (dashed, dark) AUC across three datasets (M1-M3). Error bars show standard error across 5 random seeds. Meta-learning methods like MeLU optimize over the entire parameter space Θ, which can overfit on small support sets. UNISON restricts adaptation to a low-dimensional subspace (wj , b j ) ∈ Rd′+1 ,providing implicit regularization. This trade-off is formalized by the bias-variance decomposition (E[Error ] = Bias 2 + Variance + Noise). UNISON increases bias (by restricting to linear functions) but dramatically reduces variance, yielding lower overall error in low-data regimes. We introduced UNISON, a unified framework for learning from scored bags that bridges multi-ple instance learning and recommendation systems. Our main contributions are: A) Theoretical justification for linear scoring functions (Theorem 1) and convergence rate estimate for cold-start optimization (Proposition 1). B) The introduction of functional embeddings using model parameters as bag representations in the context of MIL, and C) State-of-the-art empirical performance across diverse domains with 100 × computational speedup over meta-learning baselines. By restricting our bag-specific adaptation to a linear scoring rule, UNISON achieves a robust generalization that deep meta-learners struggle to match. Effectively, we trade expensive inference-time iterations for a stable, closed-form preference profile that captures the essence of the bag without the noise of over-parameterization. Simply stated, we move the heavy lifting of the score prediction and the bag classification to the embedding trained on all bags, and leave the simple regression task within each bag in either training or inference for the bag-specific score computation and bag classification. This leads to a trade-off on the dimension of ˜z. A high dimension could lead to higher bag classifica-tion accuracy, but would require a large support set for the estimation of w. We plan to study this trade-off theoretically and computationally. Future work will also include extending our analysis to non-Euclidean item spaces (e.g., graphs, sequences) and developing a theoretical understanding of when joint training outperforms sequential optimization. 10 References

# C Biological Applications Details 

C.1 TCR Autoencoder Architecture 

We model T cell receptor (TCR) sequences using a two-stage autoencoder-based representation learning framework. The architecture is inspired by prior work on sequence embeddings for immune receptor repertoires and is designed to capture both biochemical properties of individual clonotypes and repertoire-level structure. 

Input representation. Each CDR3 amino acid sequence is encoded as a fixed-length one-hot vector over the 20 standard amino acids with an additional stop symbol. When V-gene information is available, its categorical encoding is concatenated to the sequence representation. This produces an input vector xi for each clonotype i.

Encoder and decoder architecture. The encoder consists of two fully connected layers of width 256 with batch normalization and dropout (rate 0.232 ), followed by a three-layer latent projection head that outputs a d = 75 dimensional embedding zi ∈ R75 . The decoder mirrors the encoder with a symmetric fully connected architecture and reconstructs the original input representation ˆxi from the latent embedding. 

Stage 1: Reconstruction pretraining. In the first training stage, the autoencoder is trained solely with a reconstruction objective on the Emerson TCR dataset. Given an input sequence xi, the encoder produces a latent embedding zi, which is decoded to reconstruct the input. The reconstruction loss is defined as

Training details and hyperparameters. Autoencoder is trained using the Adam optimizer with learning rate 1.19 × 10 −4 and mini-batches of size 1000 clonotypes. The autoencoder is trained for at most 100 epochs with early stopping (patience 20, minimum improvement threshold 10 −4). Dropout is applied within the encoder with rate 0.232 .

D.1 Performance Across All Scenarios 

Table 4 shows performance on all three test scenarios. To ensure a fair comparison when using LLM-based item encoders, we additionally evaluate MeLU under two alternative embedding configurations: (1) frozen Qwen3-8B embeddings ( d=3584 ), and (2) Qwen3-8B embeddings reduced via PCA to d′=48 , matching UNISON’s latent dimensionality. Table 6 reports Spearman correlation across all cold start scenarios. MeLU’s performance degrades substantially when given raw Qwen3-8B embeddings, indicating that its gradient-based adaptation overfits in the high dimensional space. PCA partially recovers performance by reducing dimensional-ity, but does not close the gap on Book Crossing and does not surpass MeLU’s original baseline on MovieLens. This confirms that UNISON’s advantage is not merely a consequence of using stronger embeddings, but stems from its task-aware shared MLP projection ϕ, which is jointly trained to support per bag linear scoring and bag level classification.
