# Abstract 

Large-scale personalization is shifting from embedding-table retrieval to LLM-based recommenders that generate the Semantic ID (SID ) of the item to recommend, where each SID is a short sequence of discrete hierarchical content codes. Asingle instruction-tuned model can then unify retrieval, content understanding, and language, and recommend brand-new titles from their content alone using SID ;then, how should the model generate the SID ? Traditionally, models rely on a single language modeling head that generates tokens autoregressively, typically using constrained beam search. In contrast, an emerging body of work explores multi-token prediction (MTP), where dedicated heads predict all tokens in parallel, substantially reducing the decoding latency associated with beam search. We report an industry-scale, apples-to-apples comparison of these two generation paradigms : single-head autoregressive decoding vs. MTP. We introduce a new auxiliary ranking loss, defined over the joint SID score, to train the MTP objective. We evaluate the SID training objectives on both quality and training/inference cost, inside a multi-task LLM that jointly does personalized retrieval, content question-answering (QA), and instruction following over a large streaming-media catalog. Our experiments show single-head beam search consistently beats MTP on most product-critical metrics : ranking and content QA, and trains at roughly 2.6x 

lower dollar cost on Qwen3-8B scale; MTP wins only on raw inference throughput. Around this finding, we detail an efficient training and inference stack and report end-to-end gains over the previous non-SID baseline equipped with an auxiliary ranking head : +77% item cold-start ranking, +22% content-QA accuracy, +2.8x 

faster training, with language metric regression traced to SID vocabulary expansion. We address the language regression by reinforcement learning post training. 

# 1 Introduction 

Recent large-scale personalization systems increasingly leverage a large language model (LLM) to unify retrieval, ranking, content understanding, and natural-language understanding. Such an LLM ingests a member’s interaction history and context and must (i) retrieve relevant catalog entities, (ii) 

understand title content well enough to answer natural-language questions about it (content QA), and (iii) follow general instructions. We treat content understanding as a first-class training and evaluation objective alongside ranking, since it lets the model reason about why a title fits a member,

ground recommendations in metadata (not just popularity), and generalize to titles with little or no engagement history. Modern systems refer to catalog entities not by arbitrary integer ids but by Semantic IDs (SID s): short sequences of discrete codes produced by residual quantization [15, 20, 17, 18] of a content embedding, so that semantically similar titles share early-level codes. The model then generates 

the target’s SID on its own output head, which removes the catalog-sized classification head, unifies retrieval with the language and content-understanding tasks, and greatly improves cold-start because a new title inherits a meaningful (non-random) SID from its content embedding. This recipe now underlies several industrial systems (YouTube’s PLUM [7], Spotify’s GLIDE [3], Kuaishou’s OneRec [5], Meta’s generative recommenders [26]), which report substantial online gains. This raises the question at the focus of this paper: how should the LLM generate SID ? (a) Single-head autoregressive decoding emits the codes one at a time on the standard LM head with constrained beam search, which is also the classical recipe of DSI and TIGER [20, 17] and the fine-tuning stage of PLUM [7] and GLIDE [3]. (b) Multi-token prediction (MTP) attaches L dedicated heads to a shared trunk and predicts all codes in parallel [6]. Leveraging this parallel decoding, MTP can generate SID faster than the beam search in inference time. Only a few studies such as RPG [9] and STAMP [27] have applied MTP in generative recommendations. Those studies achieve higher ranking metrics or training efficiency by leveraging parallel SID prediction. However, these arguments are often made on small, retrieval-only academic benchmarks. Using an open-source model with an industry-scale streaming dataset as a controlled testbed, we reach the conclusion in the regime that matters for deployment: single-head constrained beam search beats MTP on most product-critical metrics (ranking and content QA), with a significantly lower training cost on Qwen3-8B scale. Meanwhile, MTP outperforms on inference throughput only. Our contributions are: • The industry-scale, apples-to-apples comparison of single-head autoregressive decod-ing vs. MTP as SID generation paradigms. A quality-and-cost comparison of training objectives, inside a unified multi-task LLM and large-scale streaming dataset, on ranking, content QA, language, and training/inference performance (Section 4.2). • An auxiliary ranking loss for MTP , a new objective that optimizes the item-level joint SID 

score so that MTP’s parallel heads produce calibrated, rankable item scores despite their conditional-independence assumption (Section 3.2.2). • An efficient training and inference stack : Muon [11], on-the-fly packing with cross-rank batch mixing, Cut Cross-Entropy [22], and HSDP [28] for training ( > 2× faster at −73% 

cost); and vLLM-based constrained, catalog-aware beam-search decoding for inference [13], whose trie- and catalog-level constraints guarantee every candidate names a real, request-eligible entity while prefix caching reuses the long member-history KV cache across beams (Sections 3.3–3.4). • A comprehensive evaluation and ablations covering new user and item recommendations, plus controlled ablations of LoRA [10] vs. full fine-tuning, Muon [11] vs. AdamW [16] optimizers, and content QA tasks (Section 4). 

# 2 Related Work 

Generative retrieval and semantic IDs. The Differentiable Search Index [20] first framed retrieval as sequence-to-sequence generation of document identifiers. TIGER [17] introduced semantic IDs 

for recommendation, obtaining discrete codes from an RQ-VAE [15] over item content embeddings and autoregressively generating the next item’s id. [18] show semantic IDs generalize better than random atomic ids for ranking. At industrial scale, PLUM [7] adapts a pre-trained LLM to generative retrieval via continued pre-training on SID -tokenized behavior, GLIDE [3] frames podcast discovery as instruction-following over a SID -discretized catalog, and OneRec [5] unifies retrieval and ranking in one generative model. We adopt this representation but place the SID vocabulary on a general-purpose instruction-tuned LLM’s own head, so that retrieval, content QA, and language tasks share a single decoder, rather than training a bespoke retrieval-only transformer. 2Autoregressive beam search vs. parallel/MTP decoding. MTP was introduced as an auxiliary objective that predicts n future tokens with n independent heads on a shared trunk, improving sample efficiency and enabling self-speculative decoding [6]; it is now a component of frontier LLMs [4]. In generative recommendation (GR), a recent wave applies this idea to SID s to escape the autoregressive bottleneck. RPG [9] predicts long (up to 64-token) unordered SID s in parallel with an MTP loss and graph-constrained decoding for higher efficiency and ranking quality; STAMP [27] introduces an auxiliary multi-token prediction objective to densify supervision, improving representation learning while semantic pruning reduces training cost. [23] question constrained decoding’s generalization theoretically. We revisit these conclusions empirically and at industry scale (Section 4.2): within a unified multi-task LLM, and even after strengthening MTP with an auxiliary ranking loss, single-head beam search delivers better ranking and content-QA quality at lower training cost, while MTP’s advantage is limited to inference throughput and full-catalog ranking capability 2.

Speculative and self-speculative decoding. A distinct line of work uses multi-token or draft heads purely as a lossless accelerator : Medusa [2] adds parallel decoding heads verified by tree attention, Speculative Streaming [1] folds future n-gram drafting into the target model, and [25] combine Medusa with distillation for an industrial travel application. Crucially, these methods speed up inference while preserving the underlying autoregressive model’s output distribution, so they do not trade quality for speed; they are orthogonal to, and can be layered on top of, our single-head decoder rather than being an alternative to it. NEZHA [21] makes the distinction explicit: it accelerates SID beam search with an autoregressive self-drafting head plus a hash-set verifier, and reports that a plain MTP decoder is faster but markedly worse in quality because it cannot exploit the sequential dependency inherent in the SID . We instead compare single-head vs. MTP as generation paradigms (a quality-and-cost question), whereas this line targets quality-preserving acceleration (a speed question). 

Efficient LLM training and inference. Our stack combines existing components: Muon [11], Cut Cross-Entropy [22], sequence packing [12], and HSDP [28] for both single-head and MTP training, and vLLM’s PagedAttention and prefix caching [13] for inference with the single-head and beam search. Our contribution here is not any single component but their integration and measured joint effect on a large-scale run, plus the trie- and catalog-constrained decoding layer we build on vLLM.

3.3.2 MTP: Parallel Decoding 

MTP produces all L per-code distributions from hx in a single forward pass and scores candidates by sMTP (Eq. 4); the top-K eligible entities are selected by evaluating sMTP over the trie-restricted candidate set, without sequential beam expansion. This removes the per-code forward passes of beam search, which is the source of MTP’s inference-throughput advantage. However, it also removes the autoregressive coupling crucial for accurate retrieval and ranking. Thus, we utilize the ranking loss 

Lrank to recover the retrieval/ranking ability. MTP is particularly useful for scenarios requiring dense, full-catalog scores with non-zero probability mass on every title.

# 4 Experiments 

4.1 Experimental Setup Confidentiality and training data. We study the streaming-media recommendation domain. By 

industry-scale we mean an 8B-parameter production backbone fine-tuned over a proprietary catalog on ≥ 10 M sampled examples per task drawn from production logs, evaluated under a production training- and inference-cost budget rather than on a small retrieval-only academic benchmark. To respect confidentiality we report relative rather than absolute figures for proprietary quantities (catalog size, corpus sizes, and dollar costs) and reported metrics (e.g., MRR, IFEval, MMLU). A retrieval task uses member interaction history data ( ≥ 10 M ) snapshotted at a fixed date-hour; a content QA task uses an internal content QA corpus ( ≥ 10 M ) derived from our rich content metadata; an instruction-following task uses a mix of open-source datasets[19, 14] ( ≥ 10 M ). SID s come from the latest quantizer based on RQ-VAE [17] snapshot. 

Models and baseline. All the experiments are based on open-weight Qwen3-8B backbone [24] with an optional 0.6B variant. We fine-tune such pre-trained models by supervised fine-tuning (SFT) plus LoRA [10] on our training data, and we conduct additional reinforcement learning (RL) after the SFT for language metric enhancement. We use up to 256 NVIDIA H200 and A100-80GB GPUs for our model training and inference. The baseline model in this paper refers to the previous non-SID 

model: a LoRA-only recipe with a catalog-sized auxiliary head for ranking. The additional head was necessary for the baseline to generate logits across our entire catalog. However, this ranking head can degrade training efficiency and increase maximum memory usage. 

Content QA corpus. Content QA answers natural-language questions about a title (e.g., “What is 

Movie A about?”). We internally developed a large-scale Content QA corpus in which each title is referenced by both its text and its SID , so the entity representation matches what the model sees during retrieval. The corpus is generated synthetically by prompting a frontier teacher model (e.g., GPT 5.2 ) with internal title +SID context and spans four complementary question types: title-grounded descriptions, SID -centric semantics, member-like query grounding, and two-title comparisons. To reduce sensitivity to prompt style, we sample system prompts at training time, including a 20% probability of an empty system message.

Quality: single-head wins where it counts. Single-head beam search beats MTP on all ranking and content-QA metrics (Table 1). On the Qwen 3-8B model, it improves discovery ( ↑7.3%), new user recommendation ( ↑2.1%), overall retrieval ( ↑2.0%), content understanding ( ↑2.0%), and general-language benchmarks (IFEval ↑3.3%, MMLU ↑5.7%). On the Qwen3-0.6B model, the single-head advantage is uniform and larger including gains on discovery ( ↑12.6%) and new item recommendations ( ↑6.8%). Across both scales, we attribute the ranking/QA boost of the single-head to autoregressive coupling: letting code cℓ condition on c<ℓ lets the model correct early mistakes, whereas MTP’s parallel heads must commit to all codes from a single hidden state under a conditional-independence assumption. 

Cost: single-head is far cheaper to train, but MTP is much faster at inference. The quality advantage does not come with a training-cost premium; in fact, the reverse (Table 2). For the 8B model, single-head trains 2.7× faster than MTP with 2.6× lower dollar cost, and higher model FLOPs utilization (MFU) & token per second. The only win for MTP is raw inference throughput (↑75%), consistent with the efficiency motivation of the MTP literature [6, 9]. In our deployment, this throughput edge does not offset the quality and training-cost gaps, so we adopt single-head beam search as the default and keep MTP only for applications that need dense full-catalog scores. 

Reconciling with prior work. We confirm MTP’s inference advantage but overturn the quality 

claim that parallel prediction matches autoregressive generation. Two features of our regime explain the gap: a moderate SID length ( L=4 ), where autoregressive decoding is cheap and its coupling most valuable (vs. the long L≥64 SID s that make beam search prohibitive in RPG); and multi-task training, which lets the single head share representations across retrieval, content QA, and language.

4.4 Ablation Studies LoRA vs. full fine-tuning (FT). We choose LoRA by default compared to full FT, as LoRA ranking metrics are on-par-or-better than full FT (Table 5), especially the cold-start item recommendation metric. According to Table 6, full FT is on-par-or-better than LoRA because its standard dense matrix operations avoid the extra kernel launch overheads and lower arithmetic intensity introduced by LoRA. On the 8B model, LoRA shows a language-metric drop relative to full FT because we apply LoRA to the output layer, whereas on the 0.6B model (where LoRA is not applied to the output layer) the effect reverses. We plan to revisit full FT given its simplicity and system performance. 

Muon vs AdamW optimizer. Holding everything else fixed, we compare two popular optimizers: Muon [11] and AdamW [16]. As shown in Table 7, they reach similar quality, while Muon adds a small item cold-start gain, but Muon’s system performance is clearly superior; e.g., ∼ 20% higher MFU and throughput, lower memory consumption, and larger training batch size. Thus, we set Muon as the default for both single-head and MTP training. 

Contribution of content-QA data. Table 7 tests whether content understanding helps ranking. Removing all content-QA tasks leaves ranking nearly unchanged while collapsing content QA accuracy notably. We do not observe the synergy between ranking and content QA tasks in the current SFT-only recipe. This motivates moving content QA into a CPT stage followed by ranking-focused SFT, which is a direction for future iterations, and one consistent with the CPT+SFT of PLUM [7]. 

Other ablations. We also found that introducing a separate weight for SID tokens in (2) and having the auxiliary ranking loss for MTP (5) significantly improve ranking metrics. # 5 Conclusion 

Inside a unified multi-task LLM at industry scale, single-head autoregressive SID generation with constrained beam search beats multi-token prediction on most product-critical metrics (ranking and content QA) and trains at less than half the cost on Qwen3-8B scale, while MTP leads only on inference throughput. Even after we strengthen MTP with a new auxiliary ranking loss, the single-head wins. Furthermore, the end-to-end recipe, from efficient training optimizations with SID 

to vLLM-served constrained catalog-aware beam search, more than halves training time and cost and lifts cold-start ( ↑+77%) and content-QA ( ↑+22%) over the non-SID baseline. Our findings have some limitations: (1) most experiments are based on offline training and test data, and online A/B test results might exhibit different trends, (2) the LLM could be overfit to our tasks and input format, and the output quality could be sub-optimal for diverse input format and tasks, and (3) missing continual pre-training (CPT) or mid-training stage that can warm-up the SID token embeddings and reduce the training data volume for the SFT stage. We will probe the crossover in SID shape and length, catalog size, and task mixture at which it tips. We will also extend both single-head and MTP frameworks to multi-stage training such as CPT+SFT and CPT+SFT+RL. Finally, we will diversify our input prompt style and format and utilize a larger number of tasks during the SFT, to make our LLM robust. 9References
