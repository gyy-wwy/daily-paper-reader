## Abstract 

Generative recommendation models have shown promising re-sults by representing items as semantic IDs and generating these IDs autoregressively. We introduce an approach showing that ex-plicitly incorporating hierarchical and attribute-level supervision into generative recommendation can simultaneously improve train-ing efficiency and long-tail recommendation performance. Exist-ing approaches typically optimize semantic ID prediction with a standard causal cross-entropy objective, which does not explic-itly exploit the hierarchical structure of semantic IDs and can re-quire substantial training to learn effective item representations. Moreover, data-sparse tail items often suffer from poor represen-tations due to limited supervision, resulting in weaker long-tail recommendation performance. We address these challenges with HASTE ( Hierarchical and Attribute-aware Supervision for gen-era Tive r Ecommendation), a unified training framework that in-troduces hierarchical and attribute-specific supervision. HASTE’s Hierarchical Loss explicitly exploits the multi-level structure of semantic IDs, providing supervision at each level of the item hi-erarchy and substantially accelerating training convergence. Its Attribute-Specific Losses encourage the model to preserve fine-grained item attributes, leveraging the language model’s inherent semantic knowledge to improve representations and recommenda-tions for tail items. We extensively evaluate HASTE on a fashion recommendation benchmark and across multiple domains of the Amazon dataset. HASTE improves recommendation performance across datasets and item-frequency regimes. Notably, the Hierar-chical Loss achieves approximately 2× faster convergence than the standard causal cross-entropy objective, substantially improv-ing training efficiency while maintaining strong recommendation quality.

## 1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) have triggered a paradigm shift across modalities, replacing task-specific pipelines with a single model that is pre-trained once and then adapted through instruc-tions or lightweight fine-tuning [3, 33]. Recommender systems are undergoing an analogous shift. Industrial recommenders are con-ventionally built as cascades, commonly described as matching, pre-ranking, ranking, and re-ranking [23, 27], whose stages are developed and trained as separate models with their own objec-tives: YouTube’s two-stage system, for instance, trains candidate generation as an extreme multiclass classification problem and ranking under a watch-time-weighted logistic objective [5], while pre-ranking [37] and re-ranking [25] each introduce objectives of their own. Generative recommendation instead casts the entire retrieval problem as a single sequence-to-sequence task: given a user’s interaction history, the model generates the identifier of the next item directly. [10] TIGER [26] established the form this now takes, replacing arbitrary item IDs with residual-quantized seman-tic IDs. Subsequent work has shown that ranking and retrieval can be unified in a single generative backbone [7, 40], whose quality scales as a power law of training compute [40]. A parallel line of work marries generative recommendation with LLM backbones in order to exploit the world knowledge and seman-tic priors that recommendation data alone cannot supply [11, 41]. The bridge between the two is the semantic ID (SID): rather than arXiv:2610.17368v1 [cs.IR] 12 Oct 2026 Conference acronym ’XX, June 03–05, 2018, Woodstock, NY Shukla et al. 

assigning each item an atomic, randomly initialised embedding, an item is represented as a short sequence of discrete codes de-rived from its content representation. Semantically similar items share prefixes, so the identifier space itself carries item semantics, and the model can generalise to items with little or no interac-tion history [26]. Semantic IDs are typically produced by residual quantization—either RQ-VAE [17, 26] or the training-free residual 

𝑘 -means of Quantitative Alignment Multi-Modal Recommenda-tion [21]—and are therefore hierarchical by construction . Crucially, this makes the levels asymmetric : because every later code quan-tizes the residual left by the earlier ones, an error at a coarse level redefines the target of every level below it, whereas an error at the finest level perturbs only a local refinement. Despite this structure, essentially all existing generative recom-menders supervise semantic-ID generation with a flat causal cross-entropy (CE) loss over the extended vocabulary. The consequence is not merely that the model must rediscover the coarse-to-fine or-ganisation residual quantization already encoded—it is that flat CE loss actively erodes it. We show in Section 4 that fine-tuning under flat CE cuts the cosine coupling between adjacent SID levels by 35% relative to initialization (from 0 .55 to 0 .36), while a level-aware objective retains it at 0 .50, a 9% reduction, and induces 2–4 × less embedding drift from the shared initialization. A second, orthogonal limitation is that the supervision an item receives is proportional to how often it appears as a training target. Head items accumulate enough gradient to acquire useful represen-tations; data-sparse tail items do not. This matters because semantic IDs are quantized from holistic item embeddings, so they organ-ise the catalogue by overall similarity rather than by any single attribute: a property a user actually asks for (color, material, fit, pattern) is smeared across levels rather than localised. Measuring colour directly, the coarsest code alone yields only 37% mean colour purity while conditioning on the first two codes raises it to 83%, and a probing analysis shows the information is present but not lin-early organised—a linear probe underperforms the catalogue-mean baseline at every SID position, whereas a small MLP probe reaches 

Δ𝐸 = 38 .5 against a baseline of 56 .3. The signal is latent in the representation and unexploited by the generation objective. Yet the LLM backbone already knows what a “ribbed burgundy wool cardi-gan” is: the interaction log is the bottleneck, not the backbone’s semantic knowledge. To address these limitations, we propose HASTE (Hierarchical and Attribute-aware Supervision for genera Tive r Ecommendation), a fine-tuning framework that introduce two complementary objec-tives. The Hierarchical Loss replaces flat causal CE with level-aware supervision: coarser levels carry larger base weights, and each level’s weight is gated by the model’s detached confidence in the upstream codes it is conditioned on, so fine-grained su-pervision is amplified only once the coarse decisions it depends on are actually resolved. Because confidence estimates are unre-liable early in training, the objective anneals from purely static hierarchical weighting to fully confidence-gated weighting. Sched-uled sampling [1] further perturbs the upstream prefix, exposing the model to the upstream errors it is likely to encounter dur-ing decoding rather than only to clean, teacher-forced trajectories. The Attribute-Specific Losses attach lightweight MLP regression heads to the hidden state already used to predict the fine-grained SID code, and supervise them against catalogue-derived attribute targets. A frequency-aware weighting further concentrates the sig-nal on infrequent items. We instantiate the framework for two complementary attributes—perceptual (CIELAB colour [4]) and compositional (material composition)—and find that the gains land where interaction supervision is weakest: mean top-1 colour error drops from 54 .5 to 50 .2 on tail colours and 33 .5 to 29 .0 on torso colours, while head colours are unchanged (12 .5 to 12 .6). Our contributions are as follows: 

• We propose a Hierarchical Loss that supervises semantic-ID generation level-by-level, combining decreasing level weights, detached confidence gating on upstream codes, an annealed transition from static to conditional weighting, and scheduled sampling of the upstream prefix to close the train– inference exposure gap. It exploits the hierarchy explicitly rather than leaving it to be rediscovered, preserves the in-herited structure, and yields ∼2× faster convergence than causal CE at equal or better recommendation quality. 

• We propose Attribute-Specific Losses that inject fine-grained item-attribute supervision into the generative recommender through auxiliary heads on the fine-grained SID prediction state. (Section 5.2). 

• We evaluate HASTE on three Amazon Review [13] subsets under the standard P5 5-core protocol [10], on Fashion-140k, and on an internal production-scale fashion catalog, against sequential, generative, and LLM-based baselines. HASTE gives prominent gains across datasets and item-frequency regimes, with the largest improvements on the tail items. 

## 2 Related Works 

Sequential and Generative Recommendation. Sequential rec-ommenders predict the next item from an ordered interaction his-tory. GRU4Rec [12] models sessions with recurrence, while SAS-Rec [15] and BERT4Rec [29] use unidirectional and bidirectional self-attention respectively. All three represent an item as an atomic embedding in a table that grows with the catalogue, so a rare item’s representation is learned almost entirely from its few occur-rences, and retrieval needs a nearest-neighbour index maintained separately from the model. Generative retrieval removes the in-dex: the model decodes the target’s identifier token by token [31]. GENRE [6] first cast retrieval this way, generating entity names autoregressively; DSI [32] replaced names with structured semantic identifiers obtained by hierarchical clustering; and NCI [35] added a prefix-aware decoder. P5 [10] first brought this seq2seq formula-tion to recommendation, and TIGER [26] specialised it to semantic IDs, quantizing content embeddings with RQ-VAE [17] and decod-ing the next item’s codes from the history; because those codes are derived from content and shared prefix-wise, a rare item is no longer represented in isolation. HSTU [40] showed the paradigm scales as a power law of training compute, and OneRec [7] and OneRec-Think [20] deploy it at industrial scale. In addition to the generation framework, item tokenization has also attracted consid-erable attention. LLMs for Recommendation. Improving Tail-Item Representations with Attribute Super-vision. While hierarchical supervision addresses the structure of semantic IDs, it does not explicitly ensure that the learned repre-sentations preserve individual item attributes. This is particularly important for generative recommendation, where users may ex-press preferences for specific attributes such as color, material, fit, or pattern. Semantic IDs are derived from holistic item representations and therefore organize products according to overall semantic sim-ilarity rather than any single attribute. Consequently, an attribute relevant to a user’s query may be distributed across multiple levels of the semantic-ID hierarchy and need not be explicitly preserved by the standard next-token prediction objective.

Color provides a concrete example of this limitation. In our cata-log, each item is associated with a CIE-LAB color representation, allowing us to quantify perceptual similarity between items. As shown in Figure 3, color information is distributed across the SID hierarchy: the L0 code alone achieves only 37% mean color purity, whereas conditioning on the (𝐿 0, 𝐿 1) prefix increases color purity to 83%. This indicates that fine-grained color information emerges progressively across the hierarchy rather than being localized to a single SID level. Moreover, a probe analysis shows that color information is present in the model’s hidden representations but is not readily accessible through a simple linear mapping. While the linear probe performs worse than the catalog-mean baseline at all SID positions, a lightweight MLP substantially improves color prediction, achieving a Δ𝐸 of 38 .5 at L2 compared with the base-line of 56 .3. This suggests that attribute information exists in the representation but is not explicitly organized for direct attribute prediction. The limitation is particularly important for long-tail items, which receive substantially less interaction supervision. In our catalog, 15 .1% of items occur at most five times in the training data, while 23 .8% never appear in a color-specific conversation, leaving them with little or no direct color supervision. As a result, a model may learn to generate plausible semantic IDs while failing to preserve fine-grained attributes needed to dis-tinguish similar items. These observations motivate an additional source of attribute-specific supervision . Rather than relying solely on semantic-ID prediction to implicitly encode every item property, we explicitly en-courage the model’s hidden representations to preserve fine-grained attribute information. Using color as a concrete instantiation, we apply auxiliary supervision to the item’s catalog-derived CIE-LAB representation. As shown in Figure 3, this supervision primarily benefits data-sparse items: the mean top-1 color error decreases from 54 .5 to 50 .2 for tail colors and from 33 .5 to 29 .0 for torso colors, while head colors show essentially no improvement (12 .5 to 12 .6). This suggests that attribute supervision provides complementary information precisely where interaction-based supervision is weak-est. Importantly, the formulation is not specific to color and can be instantiated with other item attributes, such as material, fit, or pattern. 

## 5 Methodology 

We propose HASTE , a unified fine-tuning framework that improves semantic-ID-based generative recommendation through two com-plementary forms of supervision. Given a user interaction context, the model autoregressively generates an item’s semantic ID as a sequence of discrete codes obtained through residual quantiza-tion. While standard causal cross-entropy provides supervision for predicting these codes, it treats the semantic-ID sequence uni-formly and does not explicitly account for its hierarchical structure. Moreover, item-level attributes provide complementary semantic information that can be particularly valuable for learning represen-tations of data-sparse tail items. Figure 1 provides an overview of the proposed training framework. HASTE introduces two auxiliary objectives to address these lim-itations. The first is a Hierarchical Loss , which explicitly exploits the multi-level structure of semantic IDs and provides level-aware supervision during semantic-ID generation. By aligning the train-ing objective with the hierarchical organization of the semantic-ID space, the model can learn coarse-to-fine item semantics more Conference acronym ’XX, June 03–05, 2018, Woodstock, NY Shukla et al. 

effectively. The second is an Attribute-Specific Loss , which in-troduces auxiliary supervision from fine-grained item attributes. The loss encourages intermediate model representations to retain attribute-level information that may not be sufficiently captured by the semantic-ID prediction objective alone. This additional su-pervision is particularly beneficial for tail items, where limited interaction data makes it difficult to learn informative representa-tions solely from the primary recommendation objective. The overall HASTE objective combines the standard causal language-model loss with these two complementary sources of supervision: 

LHASTE = Lhier + 𝜆 attr Lattr , (5) where 𝜆 attr controls the contribution of the attribute-specific ob-jective. The hierarchical loss serves as the primary semantic-ID prediction objective, replacing the standard causal cross-entropy loss, while the attribute loss provides complementary supervision for learning fine-grained item representations. Both the backbone and the auxiliary attribute prediction head are jointly optimized.

## 5.2 Attribute-Conditioned Auxiliary Losses 

Hierarchical supervision encourages the model to preserve the coarse-to-fine structure of Semantic IDs (SIDs), but it does not ex-plicitly constrain the learned representations to retain fine-grained item attributes. This is particularly important for attributes such as color and material composition, which may be weakly super-vised by conversational interactions, especially for infrequently occurring items. To address this limitation, we introduce a generic attribute-aware auxiliary objective that encourages the hidden rep-resentation used for fine-grained SID prediction to preserve at-tribute information. The proposed objective is not specific to any particular attribute and can be instantiated for any attribute that can be represented by a suitable prediction target. We demonstrate its effectiveness using two complementary item attributes, color and material composition. Auxiliary prediction heads. For each attribute 𝑎 , we attach a light-weight MLP regression head [ 𝑓 _𝑎 : R𝑑 → R𝑑 _𝑎 ], with architecture Linear (𝑑, 64 )–GELU–Linear (64 , 𝑑 𝑎 ), where 𝑑 is the backbone hid-den dimension and 𝑑 𝑎 is the dimensionality of the attribute target. The head operates on the final-layer hidden state at the position used to predict the third-level SID token 𝐿 2. At this position, the model has already conditioned on the preceding coarse-grained codes 𝐿 0 and 𝐿 1, making it a natural location for capturing fine-grained perceptual and compositional properties of the item. 

SID-conditioned attribute targets. Attribute targets are constructed independently of the SID token vocabulary. We first join the pro-duction catalog, which associates each item with its complete SID tuple (𝑙 0, 𝑙 1, 𝑙 2, 𝑙 3), with the corresponding raw attribute annotations. Since multiple items may share the same SID tuple, we aggregate the attribute annotations of all items assigned to a tuple to ob-tain a lookup function [𝑇 𝑎 (𝑙 0, 𝑙 1, 𝑙 2, 𝑙 3) → y𝑎 ]. During training, the attribute target is therefore determined directly from the item’s complete SID, rather than from the conversation or the surface form of the user query. This allows the auxiliary objective to be applied to every valid SID-generation example, including examples whose conversational context does not explicitly mention the attribute. 

Position-aligned supervision. The attribute head operates on the final-layer hidden state at the 𝐿 2 SID prediction position, which is the same representation used by the backbone to predict the fine-grained SID token after conditioning on the preceding 𝐿 0 and 𝐿 1

codes. We choose this position because the finer-grained informa-tion captured at the 𝐿 2 level is expected to be more closely related to perceptual and compositional item attributes. As discussed in Section 4, our preliminary probing experiments provide empirical evidence that the 𝐿 2 representation contains substantially more attribute information than earlier SID positions. Thus, aligning the auxiliary supervision with the 𝐿 2 prediction position directly en-courages the representation used for fine-grained item prediction to preserve attribute-specific information.

## 6 Experiments 

Implementation Details. Each semantic ID consists of four tokens 

(𝐿 0, 𝐿 1, 𝐿 2, 𝐿 3), with a 256-entry RQ-VAE codebook at each level. Our hierarchical loss uses level-specific base weights (4.0, 3.0,

2.0, 1.0), confidence-based gating, an annealed transition from static weighting to confidence gating, and scheduled sampling to reduce the train–inference exposure gap. For attribute supervision, we use a CIE-LAB distance-based soft-label objective at 𝐿 2, restricted to candidates sharing the ground-truth (𝐿 0, 𝐿 1) prefix, and apply it to color-conditioned conversations with weight 0 .1. We further employ a hidden-state regression head to provide complementary attribute supervision. Training is performed in two stages, with embedding warm-up followed by full fine-tuning using learning rate 2 × 10 −5, effective batch size 32, and a cosine learning-rate schedule. We train the Qwen3-8B model in two stages. Stage 1 extends the model vocabulary with semantic-ID (SID) tokens and initializes their embeddings. Stage 2 performs full-parameter su-pervised fine-tuning (SFT), initialized from the Stage 1 checkpoint. All experiments were conducted on NVIDIA RTX6000 single GPU instance with 96 GB memory. Additional implementation details and hyperparameters are provided in the Supplementary Material. 

Datasets. We evaluate HASTE across diverse recommendation domains and data regimes. We use four public domains from the Amazon Reviews benchmark [13] – Sports & Outdoors (Sports), Beauty, Toys & Games (Toys), and Fashion-140k, a 140K-item subset of the larger Amazon Fashion dataset, and an internal production-scale proprietary fashion dataset (internal-fashion). Sports, Beauty, and Toys follow the standard P5 5-core protocol [10], where each user and item has at least five interactions, and are used to evaluate the effectiveness of hierarchical supervision for sequential genera-tive recommendation. Fashion-140k and internal-fashion dataset provide richer item-level attribute annotations, including color and material, enabling evaluation of the proposed attribute-specific su-pervision, particularly in the long-tail setting. Across datasets, item representations are encoded as hierarchical semantic IDs using a 3-level RQ-VAE with 256 codes per level, with a deduplication token appended where applicable. Further details on dataset statistics, preprocessing, semantic-ID construction, and attribute availability are provided in the supplementary material. 

Baselines. We compare HASTE against representative baselines spanning three categories: traditional sequential recommendation methods (BERT4Rec [29], SASRec [15]), generative recommenda-tion methods (TIGER [26], HSTU [40]), and LLM-based methods (ID-GenRec [30], OneRec-Think [20]). We additionally compare against the standard cross-entropy (CE) objective to isolate the impact of our proposed supervision. 

Evaluation Protocol. We use Recall@K and NDCG@K with 𝐾 ∈{5, 10 } to evaluate recommendation performance. We evaluate the hierarchical loss on the sequential recommendation task, while attribute-specific losses are evaluated on attribute-conditioned prod-uct recommendation, both overall and on torso and tail items with fewer than 500 instances.

## 7 Conclusion 

We presented HASTE, a unified framework for generative recom-mendation with hierarchical and attribute-aware supervision. By exploiting the multi-level structure of semantic IDs and incorporat-ing confidence-aware gating, level weighting, and scheduled sam-pling, HASTE improves hierarchical prediction. Attribute-specific auxiliary objectives further preserve fine-grained item information, particularly for tail items. Experiments across multiple Amazon domains and a large-scale fashion dataset show consistent improve-ments over strong baselines, with ablations confirming the comple-mentary benefits of the proposed components. ## 8 Ethical Considerations 

HASTE is designed to improve generative recommendation and does not directly use sensitive user attributes. However, recommen-dation systems can amplify existing popularity biases and may dis-proportionately favor frequently interacted-with items, potentially reducing exposure to less popular or tail items. HASTE explicitly incorporates attribute-aware supervision to improve tail-item repre-sentation, but this does not guarantee fairness across all products or user groups. In addition, the use of catalog and behavioral data may introduce privacy concerns depending on the data collection and deployment setting. In practice, appropriate data anonymization, access controls, and privacy-preserving data practices should be applied. Finally, the framework should be evaluated for unintended recommendation biases before deployment, particularly in settings where recommendations can influence user choices or commercial outcomes.

Deduplication and sequence construction. Three deduplication passes remove system-generated duplicate events while preserving genuine repeated actions: ambiguous sessions containing multi-ple user-profile combinations are removed, exact row duplicates are discarded, and interaction-level duplicates are removed only when they match simultaneously under both session/position and timestamp-based windowing criteria. Interactions are then grouped by user-profile pair and ordered chronologically. Sequences have a minimum length of three and are capped at 100 items. Consecutive events separated by 30 days or more are discarded. 

Training-pair generation. Six interaction-pattern families gen-erate conversational training examples: cross-product transitions, same-product persistence, forward engagement, persona-conditioned prediction, backward engagement, and cross-signal escalation. Tem-poral gaps are bucketed into same-session ( <30 min), within-day (<24h), within-week ( <7d), and within-month (7–30d) intervals. This process produces 6,799,008 conversations, split into 6,255,087 training, 339,950 validation, and 203,971 test examples. 

Attributes. The Internal-fashion catalog provides brand, price, currency, gender, color, size, materials, category name, taxonomy, and VLM/attribute-assembled product descriptions. This attribute richness makes Internal-fashion, together with Fashion-140k, the primary setting for evaluating attribute-specific supervision. 

9.1.4 Semantic-ID Construction. We construct semantic IDs us-ing a TIGER-style [26] residual vector quantization autoencoder (RQ-VAE). For Sports, Beauty, and Toys, title, description, cate-gory path, and brand are concatenated into a single context string and encoded into a 1024-dimensional representation. Fashion-140k uses the RQ-VAE checkpoint trained on the full 799,953-item Ama-zon Fashion pool; semantic IDs for its 141,300 items are obtained by nearest-codebook assignment in the same embedding space. Internal-fashion additionally incorporates multimodal product rep-resentations using 1024-dimensional Qwen3-VL embeddings. The RQ-VAE encoder follows a 1024 → 512 → 256 → 128 → 32 architecture and uses three residual codebooks with 256 entries each. For Sports, Beauty, and Toys, the RQ-VAE is trained for 20,000 steps per category on a single A100 GPU. A fourth deduplication token is appended to the three quantized codes when collisions occur, producing the final depth-4 semantic ID used for language-model fine-tuning. 9.1.5 Training-Data Construction. 

Hierarchical-loss training. The hierarchical loss is a training ob-jective rather than a separate data-generation pipeline. It is applied to the same semantic-ID conversational training corpus used by the flat causal CE baseline, reweighting the per-level losses without changing the underlying examples. For Sports, Beauty, and Toys, conversational SFT data are gener-ated from item metadata, semantic IDs, and user sequences using fixed instruction templates. Sports and Beauty use 26 conversation types, while Toys uses 22 because several types requiring lower-coverage fields are omitted. Repeated instructions mapping to the same SID are intentionally retained to reinforce the SID-item map-ping and provide surface-form diversity. Examples are shuffled with a fixed seed and split 92/5/3 into training and validation. The conversation types fall into five groups: 

• SID →text decoding: recover title, description, context, cat-egory, or brand from a semantic ID. 

• Text →SID encoding: predict a semantic ID from title, de-scription, or brand information. 

• Sequential next-item prediction: predict the next item from histories of length 2, 3, 5 and all. 

• Co-engagement: predict co-purchased, frequently bought, or similar products. 

• Category/prefix structure understanding: reason over shared semantic-ID prefixes and their associated categories or examples. This produces 2,383,517, 1,634,627, and 1,320,505 training con-versations for Sports, Beauty, and Toys, respectively. 

Attribute-loss training. The attribute-conditioned loss is evalu-ated on Fashion-140k and Internal-fashion, where structured at-tributes are sufficiently dense. The loss is applied as an auxiliary objective over the training corpus rather than being restricted ex-clusively to attribute-conditioned conversations. 

Fashion-140k. The 1,336,658-example training corpus is orga-nized into five families: 

• AF1 – SID ↔text/attribute mappings: approximately 700K examples covering SID-to-text, text-to-SID, and attribute-to-SID mappings. 

• AF2 – attribute-based retrieval: approximately 27K exam-ples using gender, product type, color, style, occasion, brand, and material, including single-, two-, and three-attribute queries. 

• AF3 – sequential prediction: approximately 200K next-item and co-purchase examples. 

• AF4 – outfit color coordination: approximately 1K exam-ples derived from Fashionpedia and Polyvore color-compatibility rules. 

• AF5 – semantic hierarchy understanding: approximately 117K examples based on SID-prefix clusters and similar-item relationships. AF2 and AF4 provide the most direct attribute supervision be-cause their targets are explicitly organized by shared attributes such as color, material, gender, and style. 

Internal-fashion. The Internal-fashion SFT corpus contains 368,482 training and 19,482 validation conversations using the same 57-type schema as the underlying pipeline. In addition to SID ↔text tasks, it contains exhaustive attribute-conjunction retrieval exam-ples over combinations of brand, color, material, gender, and cate-gory. Examples include queries for all products matching a particu-lar color-category or brand-color combination. Rather than restricting the auxiliary objective to these explic-itly color-named examples, the color loss is applied as a soft per-example reweighting over the entire corpus. Examples whose con-versation type contains “color” receive the full auxiliary weight (𝛼 = 0.1), while all other examples receive a reduced scale of 0 .2𝛼 .Thus, even items that do not occur in explicitly color-conditioned conversations receive a weaker color-anchoring gradient. The color objective uses soft-label cross-entropy over color-similar candidates, with similarity determined by exp (− Δ𝐸 /𝑇 ) in CIE-LAB [4] color space and an additional frequency-based weight-ing term that upweights rare items. This formulation allows at-tribute supervision to provide stronger fine-grained signals while retaining the broad conversational training distribution.

Annealed confidence blending. The coefficient 𝛼 controls the tran-sition from static weighting to confidence-based weighting. It is initialized to 1 .0, corresponding to purely static weighting, and lin-early decreases to 0 .0 over 300 ,000 optimizer steps. Thus, training initially emphasizes a stable hierarchical curriculum and gradually transitions to adaptive supervision based on the model’s confidence in preceding levels. 

Scheduled sampling. We additionally use scheduled sampling to reduce the exposure gap between teacher-forced training and autoregressive inference. A first forward pass, performed without gradient computation, samples the predicted code at the coarsest SID level from the model’s categorical distribution. With probability 

ss _prob , this prediction replaces the corresponding ground-truth code in the input sequence. A second forward pass then computes the hierarchical loss on the resulting sequence. The scheduled-sampling probability is linearly increased from 0 to 0 .3 over 200 ,000 optimizer steps. Multinomial sampling is used rather than greedy decoding to expose the model to the stochasticity of autoregressive generation. 

Category-specific configurations. The hierarchical-loss mecha-nism is shared across all three datasets, with category-specific dif-ferences in the number of SID levels and their base weights. Sports, Beauty and Toys use four SID codes with weights (4.0, 3.0, 2.0, 1.0).The fourth code corresponds to the deduplication code in addition to the three residual-quantization levels. All other hierarchical-loss hyperparameters are shared across datasets, as summarized in Table 10. 

Training and inference. Training is performed in two stages. First, only the newly introduced SID embeddings are optimized for 50 ,000 steps using flat cross-entropy with a learning rate of 1 × 10 −3. The resulting checkpoint initializes the second stage, in which all model parameters are jointly fine-tuned using the hierarchical loss and the configuration in Table 10. During evaluation, autoregressive generation is constrained to catalog-valid SID sequences using a prefix-tree logits processor constructed from up to 100K training sequences. If a generated prefix is not present in the tree, decoding falls back to a per-position vocabulary mask over the 256 valid SID codes at the corresponding level.
