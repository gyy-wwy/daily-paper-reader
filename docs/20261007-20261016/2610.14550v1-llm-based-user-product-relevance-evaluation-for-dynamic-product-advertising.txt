## Abstract 

Dynamic Product Advertising (DPA) retrieval systems are a core component of modern e-commerce advertising; however, their eval-uation remains challenging. Conventional offline engagement met-rics are limited by sparse, biased feedback, while online evaluation is costly and time-consuming. In this work, we propose a user– product relevance metric for offline evaluation of DPA retrieval systems. The metric first compresses the engagement history into a compact set of intent representatives via Semantic-ID-grouped, event-stratified segmentation, then assesses the relevance of a rec-ommended product to each individual intent and aggregates them as a user-level relevance score, yielding a stable and interpretable mea-sure of retrieval quality. To compute this metric at scale, we leverage large language models (LLMs) as semantic relevance judges, cap-turing nuanced functional and semantic relationships that heuristic methods often miss. We propose an agentic, human-aligned prompt-ing framework that generates multi-category relevance guidelines and distills teacher-model judgments into an efficient student model for scalable evaluation. The resulting metric strongly correlates with offline and online business metrics, enabling faster and more reliable DPA retrieval iteration before A/B testing.

## 1 Introduction 

Modern E-commerce advertising platforms increasingly rely on high-quality Dynamic Product Ads (DPA) to deliver personalized user experiences at scale. Within these systems, the major task of retrieval stage is efficiently filtering a massive product catalog into a small subset of candidates based on diverse user interests and engagement histories. DPA retrieval systems typically rely on multiple algorithms to retrieve candidate products to ensure diver-sity. Although the top-line business objective is to maximize user engagement and revenue, the industry lacks a universal and reliable metric to compare the effectiveness of different retrieval algorithms. Common offline evaluation metrics such as the recall of future engagements [19] are limited by selection bias and data sparsity. Unlike in the Search domain, where explicit queries provide a clear benchmark for success, the advertising domains lack a well-defined framework for user-product relevance [1]. Consequently, machine learning practitioners often struggle to quantify whether a retrieval model is successfully surfacing relevant products or merely exploit-ing noisy, short-term behavioral correlations that may not lead to sustainable long-term growth. arXiv:2610.14550v1 [cs.IR] 10 Oct 2026 CIKM ’26, November 07–11, 2026, Rome, Italy Wen Zhang et al.

Defining and measuring relevance is the core obstacle. Prior work either uses explicit user studies that rate usefulness, trust, or satisfaction [9, 12], or implicit engagement logs of clicks, likes, shares, and reports [19]. User studies are expensive and slow [12]. Engagement is abundant but noisy: clicks are biased by presen-tation and trust effects [5], and engagement-optimized ranking amplifies attention-grabbing content users do not actually prefer when asked [6]. We instead use a content-based definition of rele-vance: an LLM judges each retrieved product against intents inferred from the user’s engagement history, and intent-level scores are aggregated into a user-level metric. This reduces de-pendency on exposure artifacts, covers users with sparse-feedback, and makes the relevance score interpretable at the attribute level. Recent work uses LLMs as semantic relevance judges for search and recommendation, scoring content alignment directly rather than relying on engagement signals [2, 10, 14, 15]. Sun et al. [14] show that prompted LLMs are effective zero-shot re-rankers and can outperform strong supervised baselines; Choi et al. [2] report substantial agreement between LLM evaluators and human raters; and large-scale benchmarks find that LLM-generated relevance labels broadly track human judgment at scale [10]. These works target search-style relevance with an explicit query. In contrast, DPA retrieval system do not have search queries and typically require inferring user-level relevance signals from heterogeneous engagement histories. We propose an offline evaluation framework that uses LLM judges to score user–product relevance for DPA retrieval. Two operational problems shape the design: the engagement history is heterogeneous and long, and frontier LLMs are too expensive to call on every event–candidate pair at production volume. We address both with (i) an SID (Semantic ID [13])-grouped, event-stratified compression of engagement history into a small set of weighted intent representatives, and (ii) an agentic guideline-discovery loop together with instruction-guided teacher-student finetune for pro-duction serving. Our contributions are 3-folds as follows: 

• Query-free relevance metric. We define a user-level offline evaluation metric for DPA retrieval that aggregates LLM-judged intent–candidate relevance under a threshold-𝑘 aggregation rule, without requiring an explicit query. 

• A Scalable LLM-judge Pipeline. We propose an efficient LLM judge pipeline with 1) SID-grouped intent decomposition that reduces LLM calls by 30 ×, and 2) teacher-student distillation from an agentic, human-vetted guideline that replaces the frontier teacher with an efficient student at production cost. 

• Online validation. The metric ranks six retrieval sources consis-tently with conversion precision/recall - how well the retrieved candidates aligns with user’s subsequence purchases - and is di-rectionally aligned with online CTR/CVR deltas across four A/B tests; It thus provides a reliable view of retrieval system health and a low-cost signal for guiding offline model iteration. 

## 2 LLM-Based User-Product Relevance Metric 

The proposed relevance evaluation framework has three blocks (Figure 1): a compact user intent representation that segments en-gagement history into SID-grouped representatives (§2.1); an intent-product relevance judge built from an agentically-discovered guide-line (§2.2.1) and a teacher-student distillation (§2.2.2); and a metric-computation layer (§2.3) that aggregates pairwise judgments into request- and user-level scores. ## 2.2 Intent-Product Relevance Judge 

Given the intent set 𝑑 𝑟 from §2.1, we need a judge J ( 𝑖, 𝑐 ) that assigns a relevance label to each (intent representative 𝑖 , candidate product 𝑐 ). Two issues shape the design. The taxonomy must cap-ture DPA-specific product semantics without becoming so detailed that a frontier LLM applies it inconsistently; and running a frontier LLM as J at production volume is too expensive. We address the first with an agentic guideline-discovery loop followed by a human-alignment pass (§2.2.1), and the second with knowledge distillation into a small student (§2.2.2). 

2.2.1 Agentic Guideline Generation. We build the evaluation guide-line in two phases. Phase A is an agentic loop where an LLM judge iteratively refines its own decision rules from labeled examples; Phase B is a human alignment pass where editors steer the resulting rule set toward DPA business priorities. 

Phase A: agentic rule discovery. We start from a minimal criteria-only seed prompt – a single instruction, “ Label the candidate as Exact, Similar, Substitute, Complementary, or Irrelevant ” – with no per-class definitions, examples, or attribute hints. A frontier LLM (e.g., Gemini-2.5-Pro) labels a stratified calibration split using this seed prompt, scored by per-class F1 against the human labels. The refinement loop iterates (i) clustering mis-classified pairs by the (predicted, label) confusion and the judge’s free-form rationale; (ii) prompting a separate reasoner to propose up to three new sub-rules targeting the true-label class for the largest cluster; and (iii) provisionally appending each sub-rule, re-labeling the calibration set, and accepting iff the paired-bootstrap 95% CI on Δaccuracy excludes zero with no per-class F1 regression beyond 0 .5 percentage points. Rejected sub-rules are logged as “previously tried”. A round ends on the first accepted sub-rule or after all proposals are rejected; the loop stops after 𝑁 rounds or two consecutive empty rounds. We run multiple trials and retain only sub-rules that recur across trials, stabilizing the discovered set against proposer stochasticity. The candidate-rule pool surfaces seven of the twelve editorial-guideline reasons (attribute-level: type, brand, model/style, size, color, ma-terial, component-vs-whole); the five not surfaced – demographic gender/age, broad-Complementary/Irrelevant, asymmetric Com-plementary – are deferred to Phase B. 

Phase B: human business-alignment. The agentic phase optimizes calibration-set agreement but is silent on downstream business goals. Human reviewers audit the Phase-A output against three criteria: coverage of business-critical mismatches (demographic gender/age, regulated categories, advertiser exclusions), editorial-standard compatibility, and reproducibility on a 200-pair business-stratified sample. Vetoed rules return to Phase A with an explana-tory note. The most consequential intervention in our deployment promoted demographic mismatch from a Substitute -leaning signal to a hard Irrelevant override – a rule the loop repeatedly depriori-tized because demographic-mismatch pairs are statistically rare in the calibration set. Phase B is load-bearing: demographic-override rules emerge from editorial review rather than from the agentic loop, because demographic-mismatch pairs are too rare in the cali-bration set to drive the loop’s statistical gate. 

Final taxonomy and rationale format. The resulting guideline – the seed taxonomy refined by Phase-A sub-rules under the Phase-B business overrides – is what the rest of the paper refers to as “the guideline”. It instructs the LLM judge to assign each (intent, candidate) pair to one of five tiers and to emit a structured ratio-nale by selecting from a controlled attribute vocabulary ( Product Type , Brand , Demographics , Component-vs-Whole , etc.). The final taxonomy summarization: 

• Exact: matches all specific attributes of the intent (type, brand, model, size, color). 

• Similar: same product category, differs in brand, model, or color. 

• Substitute: different type but functionally interchangeable. 

• Complementary: different type, typically used together with the intent [4]. CIKM ’26, November 07–11, 2026, Rome, Italy Wen Zhang et al. 

• Irrelevant: no functional or categorical link, or a critical demo-graphic mismatch. 

2.2.2 Teacher-Student Knowledge Distillation. The finalized guide-line from §2.2.1 could in principle be deployed as an instruction prompt to a frontier LLM at serving time, but at production vol-ume this is prohibitively expensive (Table 5). We instead use the guideline-prompted frontier LLM as a teacher that emits high-quality labels and rationales on a curated training corpus, then fine-tune a much smaller student LLM on those labels. The student is what we deploy as J .

Distillation dataset construction. We construct a large distilla-tion corpus that reflects real-world retrieval scenarios via a four-stage pipeline. Stratified seed sampling: intent representatives 𝑖 𝑔 

are sampled stratified across user engagements and demographics (age, gender, engagement type, engagement frequency), ensuring broader and more balanced pattern coverage than a uniform ran-dom distribution. Multi-source candidate generation: for each seed, candidates are aggregated from three complementary streams – production retrieval logs, historical conversion-driven engage-ment signals, and curated high-value advertiser groups. Hard-case mining: we downsample to 𝑁 = 10 candidates per seed using a multimodal embedding filter to intentionally surface semantically-similar-but-irrelevant negatives. Labeling: pairs are labeled by the teacher protocol above; rationale traces are retained as auxiliary supervision for the student. 

Teacher labeling. The finalized guideline is loaded as the instruc-tion prompt to a frontier LLM (Gemini-2.5-Pro), which annotates each seed–candidate pair in the distillation datasets with a relation label and an attribute-level rationale. GPT-5 runs in parallel under the same guideline as a verification agent; pairs on which the two agents agree at high self-reported confidence are accepted directly, and the disagreed cases are sent to human auditors for relabeling. 

Student fine-tuning. We test both Gemini-2.5-Flash and Gemini-2.5-Flash-Lite as student backbones and apply parameter-efficient instruction fine-tuning (LoRA with adapter rank 16, learning-rate multiplier 2, 5 epochs). The student is trained for multi-label classi-fication over the five-tier taxonomy and jointly emits an attribute-level rationale conditioned on the label, matching the teacher’s output format. At serving time, the student replaces both teacher LLMs in the inference path; the teacher is invoked only on guideline-update or distillation-refresh cycles.

## 3 Online-Offline Retrieval Metrics Alignment 

In this section, we benchmark the proposed LLM-based user-product relevance metric against conventional DPA retrieval offline met-rics (e.g., Conversion Precision/Recall [19]) and evaluate their consistency across various retrieval sources. A key advantage of the LLM-based approach is its independence from online traffic data. While conventional metrics often rely on future engagement signals to accurately measure precision/recall, our metric operates strictly offline, providing a high-fidelity relevance signal without the need for real-time interaction logs. ## 3.1 Online Conversion Precision/Recall 

Utilizing online traffic, we employ a look-back evaluation strat-egy that links observed conversions to their contributing ad re-quests [19]. We define relevance using a strict exact-match crite-rion, where a retrieved product must be identical to the converted item within a one-day post-impression attribution window. This focus assesses the system’s ability to surface the precise product driving a transaction. Accordingly, Conversion Precision ( CP ) is the proportion of retrieved products that match the conversion, while Conversion Recall ( CR ) measures the fraction of ultimately converted products successfully surfaced by the system. Metrics are limited to users with both an eligible ad request and a subsequent confirmed conversion. 

## 3.2 Retrieval Source Baselines 

A production DPA retrieval system blends several heterogeneous sources, each with distinct algorithmic and signal-coverage profiles, so a single offline metric that ranks them consistently must gen-eralize across signal types. To make our evaluation reproducible and to expose where each source is expected to win or lose, we describe the six representative retrieval sources used throughout this study. Matched Features (MF) is a deterministic retargeting source: candidates are products whose canonical IDs appear in the user’s recent engagement log, optionally expanded by the seller’s product-graph siblings. Coverage is narrow but precision is high by construction. Similar User (SU) is a two-tower collaborative model trained on (𝑢, 𝑝 ) engagement pairs with sampled softmax; at serving time the user tower’s vector is used to retrieve the top items from an ANN index over the global active catalog. Content-Based Retrieval (CBR) encodes each catalog item with an in-house mul-timodal encoder (image + title + structured attributes) and serves products that are nearest neighbors of the user’s recently engaged items in this content embedding space. Engagement-Based Re-trieval (EBR) is a sequence-aware retrieval tower fine-tuned on impression-attributed engagement; it shares the same ANN backend as CBR but its encoder is trained on behavioral co-occurrence rather than content similarity, so it captures complementary patterns such as cross-category purchase pairs. Popularity-Based Retrieval (PBR) ranks the global catalog by a smoothed conversion rate over the trailing 𝑁 d, segmented by coarse demographic buckets; it has no per-user signal beyond the segment. The Random Baseline (Rand) samples products uniformly from a coarse category bucket aligned with the user’s last engaged category. All sources retrieve a top-𝐾 =15 candidate set per request; the same downstream ranker and budget policy is applied so observed differences are attributable to the retrieval stage.

## 4 Ablation Study 

We construct a 10K dataset from retrieval logs, audited through multiple rounds of human review to reach consensus. To ensure reliable labels, we adopt a multi-agent framework in which GPT-5 and Gemini-2.5-Pro independently generate judgments that are aggregated via confidence scores; human annotators resolve dis-agreements until convergence, and the final judgments are treated as ground truth. Results of the main ablation are shown in Ta-ble 4. In addition, we ablate two design choices that are central to the framework but whose empirical justification would have inter-rupted the methodology in §2.1 and §2.2.1: the SID-based intent-set construction (§4.1), and the two-phase agentic-then-human guideline-generation procedure (§4.2).

## 4.2 Impact of Guidelines 

Using Gemini-2.5-Pro as the backbone, a judge given only brief relevance definitions (without the guideline) is 4% worse on both precision and recall than the guideline-prompted version (Table 4) and even underperforms the smaller student model with guide-line, showing that the guideline carries most of the lift. The gain concentrates on rule classes that the loose prompt systematically misjudges, e.g., demographic mismatches and brand-vs.-category confusions, which the agentic discovery loop in §2.2.1 surfaces.

## 4.4 Intent-Aggregation Threshold 𝑘 

We compare the production default 𝑘 =7 against the at-least-one permissive baseline ( 𝑘 = 1), which equates a single weak intent match with broad intent coverage. We sweep 𝑘 ∈ [ 1, 15 ] on the same exhaustive event–candidate judgment matrix used in §4.1 so the only varying quantity is the aggregation rule. Figure 2 reports the Pearson correlation between the resulting MicroRel and CP, CR, and CVR measured per-(date, source) over a 30-day production window across 2 retrieval sources. All three correlations reach their maxima within 𝑘 ∈ [ 5, 7]: P(CP) peaks at 0 .43 ( 𝑘 =5), and P(CR) and P(CVR) both peak at 𝑘 =7. Beyond 𝑘 ≥ 8, P(CP) and P(CR) decay as the candidate pass-rate drops below ∼5%, while CVR retains most of its signal through 𝑘 =13. [+ 0.29 , +0.68 ]. The intuition is that high-𝑘 aggregation filters out single weak intent matches that are merely category-coincidental, requiring multi-intent support before a candidate counts as relevant, which aligns better with multi-touch purchase behavior. 

## 5 System Efficiency 

We benchmark four judge configurations on Vertex AI at 16-thread concurrency over 100 production retrieval pairs (Table 5). The fine-tuned Gemini-2.5-Flash we deploy reaches 751 ms p50, 1 ,117 ms p95, and 18 QPS – roughly 9 × faster than the teacher on p50 latency and on throughput. The fine-tuned Gemini-2.5-Flash-Lite variant offers a marginal further gain at 647 ms p50 and 20 QPS. Most of the latency improvement therefore comes from fine-tuning rather than from picking a smaller backbone: prompt-only Flash still in-herits the teacher’s long-tail p95 (8 ,807 ms), whereas the fine-tuned student compresses both p50 and p95 by an order of magnitude.

The offline-online evaluation gap in e-commerce recommendation has been documented at multiple scales [8]. Counterfactual estimators (IPS, doubly robust) reduce some of this bias [5] but assume access to logged propensities that are rarely available at the retrieval stage of a multi-tower ad system. Our work is complementary: rather than debiasing engagement labels, we replace them with a content-grounded relevance label produced by an LLM judge, and report alignment with engagement-based offline metrics. 

LLM judges for relevance. LLM-as-judge has moved from open-domain summarization [14] to e-commerce and recommender rel-evance labeling: LREF [15] fine-tunes an LLM as a query-product relevance scorer with chain-of-thought, BloomIntent [2] performs intent-conditioned evaluation of SERP satisfaction, Wang et al. [18] progressively train an LLM-based semantic-ads retrieval encoder at Walmart, Penha et al. [7] examine LLM-judge ranking agreement with humans in Cranfield-style movie and podcast recommenda-tion, and large-scale studies show LLM labels broadly track human judgment in IR settings [10, 17]. Two gaps remain. First , these works rely on either an explicit query (search) or a Cranfield-style topic per request (movie/podcast collections); DPA retrieval has neither – intent must be inferred from heterogeneous engagement signals, so we propose SID-grouped, event-stratified intent decomposition as the compression rule over the engagement history. Second , prior LLM-judge studies validate against human assessors at the pair or ranking level; we instead validate against online business KPIs (CTR, CVR, conversion P/R) on the launch-decision critical path. 

## 7 Conclusion and Limitation 

We present a query-free user–product relevance metric for offline evaluation of DPA retrieval. A user’s engagement history is com-pressed into a small SID-grouped, event-stratified intent set, and an LLM judge scores each retrieved candidate against this set; an agentically-discovered guideline anchors a Gemini-2.5-Pro teacher, distilled into a Gemini-2.5-Flash student that matches the teacher at ∼9× lower latency. On 30 days of production DPA traffic, the metric ranks six retrieval sources consistently with conversion precision/recall and is directionally aligned with online CTR/CVR across four A/B tests; it serves as the offline gate for DPA model evaluation before online deployment.
